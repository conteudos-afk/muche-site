The question almost always arrives straight after the other one. Someone has worked out what a brand video costs, found the number high, and asks: and for social, the short things, how much is that?

The honest answer is that it costs considerably less. But the discount doesn't come from being shorter. It comes from being simpler: fewer days, fewer people, less preparation, fewer layers of post-production. And there's one condition that determines everything else — the video has to have been conceived for that format from the start. A vertical crop of something filmed horizontally for the website isn't a social video. It's the website video with the edges chopped off, and it shows.

## Why it costs less

A brand video carries a set of demands that come from where it's going to live. It sits on the website for years, opens presentations, gets shown to investors, represents the company in contexts where nobody knows it. That imposes a production standard that doesn't tolerate shortcuts: built lighting, clean sound, an image that holds up on a large screen.

A social video lives somewhere else. It's watched on a phone, often with the sound off, between dozens of other things, for a few seconds. This doesn't mean it can be bad — it means the definition of good is different. What matters is the clarity of the idea in the first seconds, legibility on a small screen, and the ability to work with captions instead of audio. Meticulous colour grading changes none of that. A well-chosen opening line changes everything.

From this come legitimate cuts in cost:

- **Fewer hours of preparation.** You still need to know what you're going to say and why, but you don't need a script with scenes, locations and multiple participants.
- **A smaller crew.** Plenty of social formats are handled by two people, sometimes one, provided the setup is simple and the sound is under control.
- **Less shooting time per piece.** The real economy appears when you film several pieces on the same day, in the same setting, under the same light.
- **Shorter post-production.** Edit, captions, basic colour correction. No elaborate graphics, no complex sound mix.

## Where the price moves

The variables are the same as in any production, just at a different scale. They're worth looking at one by one, because this is where two quotes differ.

### How many pieces come out of the same day

It's the most important variable and the one almost nobody uses. The fixed cost of a shoot day — travel, setup, lighting, derig — is paid once. If one piece comes out of that day, the cost per piece is the cost of the day. If eight come out, you divide by eight.

This changes how you plan. Instead of asking for one video at a time across the year, you gather the whole list, decide what can be filmed in the same place with the same person, and do it as a block. The creative work of thinking through eight pieces is greater than thinking through one, but the logistical work is almost identical. That's where the saving sits.

There's a practical limit: nobody records eight usable pieces in an afternoon if the person on camera has never spoken to a lens before. The stamina of whoever is talking runs out long before the equipment's does.

### The level of production ambition

A well-lit static shot of someone speaking is one thing. A sequence with movement, a change of setting, a product being handled and two or three angles is another. Both can be social videos and both can work — but one takes twenty minutes and the other takes a morning.

The decision about ambition should come from the job the piece has to do, not from taste. An advert with media budget behind it justifies more production, because far more people will see it and because it competes with ads made by people who invest in exactly that. An organic post that will live three days in the feed doesn't justify the same.

### Graphics and captions

Captions aren't an extra: in a format consumed with the sound off, they're part of the video. The cost varies with what you do with them. Automatic captions tidied up by hand are quick. Captions set in the brand's typeface, paced word by word and animated, are real editing work, and they multiply the post-production hours.

The same goes for title cards, straps and graphic elements. If there's already a built graphics system — a set of pieces in the brand's typography and colours, ready to reuse — each new video gets cheaper. If it has to be invented every time, it doesn't.

### The output formats

One piece delivered in vertical is one piece. The same piece delivered vertical, square and horizontal, at three different durations, is six edit jobs. It isn't six videos, but it isn't one either.

This is easy to forget in the brief and hard to forget on the invoice. Decide beforehand where the piece is actually going to circulate and cut the rest. The square version nobody asked for is time paid for nothing.

### Music and rights

A library track with a commercial licence has a small cost per piece, but it has a cost. The popular music doing the rounds on social is not usable by a brand without licensing — the platforms' audio libraries have different terms for business accounts and personal accounts, and assuming that what your nephew does on his phone applies to the brand is a cheap way to get the video taken down.

## The expensive mistake: cutting the big video into pieces

The idea sounds sensible. You've already paid for a two-minute brand video, there's plenty of footage, so you ask for three short social versions out of it. It costs little, it uses what already exists, it looks efficient.

In practice it almost always comes out badly, for four reasons worth understanding before you make the call.

**The framing isn't the same.** A shot composed for 16:9 has its information spread horizontally. Crop to vertical and you lose the sides — and the sides are where half the composition was. You end up with the person off-centre, the product out of frame, the logo running off the edge. There are ways to disguise it: scale the image up, reframe shot by shot, use a blurred background top and bottom. All of them either degrade the image or give away where it came from.

**The rhythm isn't the same.** A brand video can afford to open slowly, because whoever is watching chose to watch it. A video in the feed gets no such courtesy. The first seconds have to say what this is about, and the structure of a long video rarely has anything usable in its first seconds — it has a handsome aerial and a logo.

**The writing isn't the same.** A sentence written to be listened to attentively, inside a two-minute video, is not a sentence written to be read as a caption on a phone. It's longer, more explanatory, more full of subordinate clauses. Cutting it produces sentences that begin halfway through an idea.

**What was needed wasn't filmed.** This is the decisive one. Short pieces need their own shots: an opening frame that grabs, enough detail to cut quickly, enough variation to sustain a fast rhythm. If nobody asked for that on the day, it isn't there.

The end result is a set of pieces that aren't badly made but that visibly were made out of something else. And unlike a bad video, this isn't fixed in the edit.

## What to do instead

If you're about to shoot a brand video and you know you also want social pieces, say so before the shoot. The cost difference is small — half an hour to an hour on the same day, with the crew already set up — and what you gain is material that was genuinely conceived for the format.

In practice that means adding a few things to the shot list:

- **Dedicated vertical shots.** Not reframing afterwards. Turning the camera, or recording simultaneously with a second one, and composing for that ratio.
- **Short lines recorded deliberately.** Ask the person to say the same idea in fifteen seconds, isolated, with no surrounding context. That's the raw material a short piece lives on.
- **Too much coverage.** Details, hands, objects, atmosphere. Short pieces eat shots at a rate that always surprises people.
- **An opening of its own.** A gesture, an action, a line that works with nothing before it.

The alternative, when there's no brand video in the equation at all, is to treat social as a standalone block with its own production and its own calendar. One day a quarter, a list of pieces decided in advance, a setting that repeats and becomes recognisable. It comes out cheaper per piece than any other way of doing it, and it has the advantage of producing consistency — which in a feed is worth more than any single handsome shot.

## The number that matters isn't the video's

When you compare quotes, don't look at the total. Look at the value per piece delivered and ask what's included in each one: formats, captions, rounds of amends, music licence.

And do the sum nobody does: the cost of the piece divided by how long it's going to live. A social video made in a rush costs little and disappears in a week. A block of well-considered pieces costs more once and feeds months of calendar. It's the same logic as any production — the right price is the one that matches the work the piece actually has to do.
