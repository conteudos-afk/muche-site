Every brand reaches a point where its visual identity no longer reflects what it has become. The design that felt fresh five years ago now looks dated. The logo that worked at launch doesn't scale into a digital context nobody was planning for. The colour palette that made sense for one market doesn't serve a wider audience. The question isn't whether you should evolve — it's how to do it without pushing away the people who already trust you.

It's worth being clear about what's at stake. A brand that's been around for a few years has accumulated something that doesn't show up in any file: the ability to be recognised without effort. Someone driving past, catching the shopfront out of the corner of their eye, knowing what it is. Someone scanning a shelf and identifying the packaging before reading the name. That recognition cost years and a lot of money in repeated exposure. A badly handled rebrand erases it in a week, and the brand goes back to square one without anybody in the room understanding that's what just happened.

## Audit before you redesign

Before you touch anything visual, understand what your brand has already built. This isn't a formality: it's the part of the work that determines whether the rest goes well.

Talk to current customers, to customers who stopped buying, to whoever answers the phone or stands behind the counter. The useful questions aren't about taste — nobody gives you good information when you ask them whether they like the logo. The useful questions are about recognition and association. How would you describe this brand to a friend. What did you expect to find here before you walked in. If you saw this in a magazine with the name removed, who would you say it was.

Then take an inventory of what actually exists. Not the idealised version in the brand guidelines, but reality: the logo on the vans, the email signature, the label printed by four different suppliers, the uniform, the invoice, the PDF the sales team sends out. It's almost always a harvest of misaligned versions, and it matters to see that mess all in one place — because half the problems that motivate a rebrand are solved by discipline in application, not by new drawing.

### What carries the value is rarely the logo

The most frequent discovery of an audit is that the element holding the most accumulated capital isn't the graphic mark. It's a colour. It's a typeface. It's a way of photographing things, a tone of voice, a pack with an unusual shape, a visual gesture repeated so often it started working as a signature.

Think about how many brands you can identify from a fragment: a band of colour on a shelf, a particular red, the silhouette of a bottle, the way headlines are set. None of those signals is the logo, and all of them work at distances and speeds where the logo can no longer be read.

Those are the elements you protect. Everything else is open to change — and there's usually far more open than closed, which is good news for anyone worried that preserving means not being allowed to touch anything.

### Separate what's old from what's badly applied

Before you accept the diagnosis that the identity is out of date, check whether it isn't simply an execution problem. A brand looks cheap when it's used at low resolution, when the palette has grown over time with colours nobody approved, when three typefaces are in circulation because somebody didn't have the licence for the original, when every campaign's photography has a different style.

None of that is fixed by a new logo. It's fixed by a tidy system and somebody willing to enforce it. If you go ahead with the redesign without correcting the cause, in three years you'll have exactly the same problem with a different drawing.

## Evolution, not revolution

The most successful rebrands are evolutionary. They take the best of what exists and refine it — better proportions, cleaner execution, a more considered palette — while preserving the DNA that made the brand recognisable. The audience may not even notice the change immediately, but they'll feel it: a sense that the brand has matured without abandoning whatever made it trustworthy.

In practice, an evolutionary rebrand nearly always works on the same things.

- **Proportion and weight.** Logos drawn for print tend to have strokes that are too fine and details that disappear at twenty pixels. Thickening them, simplifying internal joins and opening up counters is invisible work with an immediate effect on legibility on a phone and in a favicon.
- **Spacing.** Badly fitted letters are the most common symptom of a mark made in a hurry. Correcting the letter spacing of a logo doesn't change what it is; it changes the class it appears to belong to.
- **Colour for screen.** Plenty of palettes were born for ink and were never considered for dark backgrounds, for night mode, for the minimum contrast of readable text. Adjusting luminance and adding variants is modernising without betraying anything.
- **Supporting typography.** Changing the typeface across the whole system alters the character of a brand profoundly, and almost nobody can say why. It's one of the strongest levers you have with the least risk of rupture.
- **The system around it.** Grids, icons, photography, motion. This is where most of what a brand appears to be today actually lives, and it's where most older identities simply have nothing.

### When revolution is justified

There are cases where continuity is the problem, not the asset. If the business genuinely changed, if the brand inherited negative associations it doesn't want to carry, if the name is legally indefensible, or if the current identity actively excludes the audience it now serves, then preserving recognition means preserving the wrong thing.

The difference is that a deliberate break demands a far bigger communications investment — you have to rebuild recognition by force, with repetition and time. It's a budget decision as much as a design one. The dangerous mistake is running a revolution while believing you're running an evolution: changing everything at once, with no transition plan, because the studio presented an exciting proposal on a Thursday afternoon.

### Bridges for the transition

Between the two extremes there are practical tools. You can keep the colour and change everything else. You can keep the shape and change the colour. You can let both versions coexist for a period, running the old one small alongside the new. You can change digital first, where you have control and speed, and let signage and packaging follow in their natural replacement cycle.

A phased transition has one advantage that rarely gets discussed: it stops the brand appearing half new and half old purely through logistical incapacity. If the fleet is only going to be resprayed over two years, it's better to decide that than to pretend it won't happen.

## Communication is part of the work

How you launch a rebrand counts as much as the rebrand itself. Customers who feel the change was imposed on them react differently from those who feel invited into it. The narrative around why the brand is evolving — what it learned, where it's going — turns a visual update into a statement of intent.

### Start inside

The people who work with you will be the first to explain the change, whether they're prepared or not. If the sales team discovers the new logo on Instagram at the same time as customers, you'll have dozens of conversations in which your own people say they don't understand the decision. Give them context, give them the files, give them a short sentence they can repeat without having to memorise it.

### Explain the why, not the process

Nobody outside the company cares about the grid or the rationale behind the angle. They want to know what changed about the brand as a business, and what hasn't changed about it as a promise. Both halves matter, and the second is the one that reassures: saying explicitly what stays the same — the team, the product, the price, the address — stops the visual change being read as a sign that something happened behind the scenes.

### Expect the loyal ones to react

The people who like your brand most are the ones most unsettled when it changes. That's a sign of attachment, not of failure, and it passes. What doesn't pass is responding badly: getting defensive, deleting comments, treating criticism as ignorance. Acknowledging affection for the previous version costs almost nothing and resolves almost everything.

## What the change says about you

A rebrand done well is a signal of confidence, not confusion. It tells your market that you're paying attention, that you're growing, and that you take seriously the experience of anyone dealing with your brand. Done badly, it erases brand capital that took years to build — and the worst part is that the erasure shows up nowhere, because nobody measures the recognition that stopped existing.

The difference lies entirely in the process: in what was audited beforehand, in what was deliberately protected, in the pace of the transition and in the story that accompanied it. The design is the visible part. It's rarely the part that decides.
