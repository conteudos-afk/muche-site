The most persistent myth in video production is that great results come from great execution on the day. In reality, the shoot day is where you either collect or lose what you built in pre-production. By the time the camera is rolling, a well-run production is simply executing a plan that has already solved most of the problems.

This isn't a defence of paperwork. It's an observation about the nature of the medium: video is the only creative discipline where you have a fixed number of hours, on a set date, with paid people waiting, to gather raw material you can't go back for without repeating the entire expense. Anything that wasn't decided beforehand gets decided in a hurry, with half a dozen people looking at you.

## The shoot day doesn't solve problems — it reveals them

Watch what happens on a badly prepared shoot. You arrive at the location and discover the room echoes. You discover the person who was going to speak was never told they'd be speaking. You discover the window with the beautiful light faces west, and that at ten in the morning it's a grey wall. None of these problems are hard. All of them are trivial to fix a week in advance and nearly impossible to fix with the crew set up and the clock running.

What happens next is always the same: you improvise. And improvisation, in video, rarely produces the right shot. It produces the available shot. Then the edit inherits twenty available shots and no right ones, and the conversation shifts from what you can say to what you can salvage.

Pre-production isn't there to eliminate the unexpected. It's there to make sure that when the unexpected arrives, it finds a crew with room to deal with it rather than a crew already in debt.

## The script is the foundation

A video demands far more time thinking than filming. A script — whether for spoken narrative, a visual sequence or a structured interview — defines what has to be captured and what the edit is going to need. Shooting without one is asking the editor to build a house out of bricks collected at random.

Script doesn't necessarily mean written dialogue. It means somebody decided, before the day, what order the ideas go in and what holds each of them up visually. In different formats that takes different shapes:

- **Narration or voiceover.** Write the text first and read it aloud, with a stopwatch. Text that looks fine on the page is usually too long and too written when spoken. And only once you've timed it do you know how many shots you need to cover it.
- **Wordless visual sequence.** Here the script is a sequence of concrete actions, in order: the hands opening the box, the part going into the machine, the glance at the display, the finished piece on the bench. It isn't a list of themes — it's a list of things somebody is actually going to have to do in front of the camera.
- **Structured interview.** The script is the questions, written and ordered, with a clear sense of which sentence the video needs to end up with. If you don't know what sentence you're looking for, you'll leave with forty minutes of pleasant conversation and nothing to open on.

There's a useful test before you call a script finished: go through it and ask, line by line, what's on screen while this is being said. If you find thirty seconds of text with nothing to show, you don't have an edit problem — you have a script problem, and it's far cheaper to fix now.

The script also tells you what to shoot extra. The edit always needs coverage: detail shots, atmosphere, gestures, the space with nobody in it. That's the material that lets you cut a hesitation, shorten a long answer or join two sentences spoken five minutes apart. Anyone who doesn't plan for it in pre-production doesn't shoot it, because on the day there's never spare time for what nobody asked for.

## The location isn't a backdrop

The space you shoot in communicates as forcefully as the words being said. A location that genuinely belongs to the brand — a real workplace, a real environment, a place with meaning — reads differently from a rented studio dressed to look like one. Wherever possible, choose the authentic over the constructed.

But authentic doesn't mean accepting the space as it is. It means going there first. A technical recce prevents almost every shoot-day disaster, and the questions are always the same:

- **Sound.** Does the air conditioning switch off? Does the road alongside carry lorries? Does the fridge kick in every five minutes? A beautiful room with a concrete floor and bare walls gives you an echo no post-production fixes well.
- **Light.** What time does the light come through that window, and how long does it stay? If the setup takes three hours, the light at the start isn't the light at the end — and that shows in the edit.
- **Power.** Where are the sockets, and will they take what you're plugging in? How many extension leads do you need?
- **Access and timings.** Where do you unload the kit? Is there a lift? How long does it take to get everything up and set up? That hour and a half exists, even if nobody wrote it into the schedule.
- **Permissions.** Who has to authorise filming there, and have they done so in writing? In shared buildings, managed properties, shopping centres or on public streets, that question gets asked weeks ahead.

And there's the matter of whether the space is filmable at all. Real factories have safety posters, cardboard boxes and a 2019 calendar on the wall. The point isn't to tidy everything until it stops looking like somewhere people work — it's to decide, in advance, what comes out of frame and what stays because it tells the right story.

## Who appears on camera is decided beforehand

Casting a brand video is rarely casting in the classical sense. It's choosing which of the company's people are going to speak, and telling them far enough in advance that they don't arrive caught off guard.

Two things are worth settling in pre-production. First: who speaks and about what, so you don't end up with three people saying the same thing and nobody saying the thing only one of them knows. Second: whether the person you've chosen can handle a camera. Not everyone can, and it has nothing to do with competence. A conversation beforehand, with no equipment in the room, shows you who comes alive talking about the work and who closes up.

It's also worth dealing with the tedious things: the signed image release, what the person is going to wear, and whether the job title in the caption is actually the current one. Discovering someone left the company after the edit was locked is a problem you solve before, not after.

## The shot list turns the script into shots

A shot list is the translation of the script into concrete camera instructions. For each shot: what's visible, what framing, whether there's movement, what sound is needed and which part of the script it serves. It doesn't need to be a beautiful document. It needs to exist on paper, in the hand of whoever is shooting.

The real value shows up at two moments. During the day, it lets you know at any point how much is done and how much is left — and therefore when to start cutting shots deliberately, rather than cutting all of them at the end in a panic. And at the end of the day, it lets you confirm nothing is missing before the kit gets packed away. A forgotten shot only surfaces in the edit, and by then it costs a whole day.

An honest shot list is also a conversation tool with the client. When somebody asks for three more scenes the night before, the shot list shows, without argument, what those three scenes force you to leave out.

## The call sheet protects the day

A call sheet tells everyone on the production what time they need to be where, what to bring and what happens in what order. Full address, with parking noted. Contacts for whoever makes decisions on site. The time each contributor is called — not the time the crew starts, because leaving a managing director waiting for two hours is the surest way to have them tense before they speak.

It should also include what usually gets forgotten: the lunch break, the forecast if there are exteriors, and the alternative plan if it rains. A crew that knows what's coming next works slowly and well. A crew that doesn't works quickly and badly.

Neither the shot list nor the call sheet is a bureaucratic document. They're the difference between a focused, efficient day and a chaotic, expensive one.

## Plan B is also pre-production

Preparing isn't predicting everything. It's deciding in advance what to do when the three or four predictable things go wrong: rain on an exterior, someone who doesn't turn up, a machine that isn't running that day after all, an equipment failure. Each of these has a reasonable answer if it's thought through calmly, and a bad one if it's thought through on the spot.

The same logic applies to the footage. Duplicate cards, copies made while still on site, a drive that leaves in somebody else's car. The cost of reshooting is always higher than the cost of duplicating files.

And there's the least technical decision of all: who approves. A well-prepared video that passes, at the end, through five people who were never part of the conversation always comes back diluted. Knowing who has the final word — and having them on the script, before the shoot — saves edit rounds nobody budgeted for.

## Good videos are planned into existence

Investment in thorough pre-production always returns more than it costs. It reduces the risk of expensive reshoots, it gives confidence to whoever is paying for the work, and it creates the conditions for the creative team to do its best work — because a crew that isn't firefighting has attention to spare for the shot nobody planned that turns out better than all the others.

That's the irony of preparation, in the end: it isn't what kills good improvisation, it's what makes it possible. You can only take advantage of chance when everything else is already secured.

Good videos are planned into existence. They aren't improvised.
