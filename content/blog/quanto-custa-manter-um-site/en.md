There's a conversation that almost never happens at the start of a web project: what is it going to cost to have this website two years from now. You talk about pages, about deadlines, about who writes the text. The budget is agreed around the build, the site goes live, everyone breathes out — and the cost of keeping the thing alive turns up afterwards, in pieces, with no line in any budget to sit on.

Nobody's acting in bad faith. It's just that a website looks finished on the day it launches, and it isn't. It's closer to a car than to a painting: it works, it does the job, and it needs maintaining to keep doing the job. The difference is that everybody already knows a car needs servicing.

## Hosting and domain: the cost you can see

Start with the simplest part, which is also the only part most people have in mind.

The **domain** is paid once a year and costs very little. The risk isn't the amount: it's forgetting. Domains expire because the card on file ran out, because the warning email went to the inbox of someone who left the company, because nobody knew who was responsible. Recovering a domain once it's been released is somewhere between difficult and impossible, and during that window the site doesn't exist and neither does the company email.

**Hosting** varies far more, and the variation makes sense. A static brochure site can live on infrastructure that costs next to nothing. A WordPress site with a client area and steady traffic needs something with reserved resources, and that's a real jump in price. An online shop with seasonal peaks needs headroom for the peak, not for the average.

The temptation is to pick the cheapest plan. It's understandable and it usually turns out expensive, because cheap hosting is cheap for concrete reasons: servers shared with a great many other sites, high response times, slow technical support when something breaks. The cost doesn't show up on the invoice — it shows up as sluggishness and as days of downtime.

Then there are the pieces that get forgotten because they're small:

- **SSL certificate.** These days it's often free and automatic. Not always, particularly on older setups or enterprise hosting.
- **Professional email.** If your address is @ your brand, someone is paying for it somewhere, usually per user per month.
- **Third-party services.** Forms, booking, chat, maps, newsletter, cookie consent management. Each one is cheap. Together they routinely cost more than the hosting.
- **Backups.** Some hosts include them. Many include only a limited version, which isn't enough when the problem is discovered three weeks later.

It's worth sitting down once and listing everything that gets charged because of the website, with annual amounts and the name of whoever holds each account. It's a half-hour exercise and it almost always turns up something nobody has used in a year.

## Updates: the cost you don't see until it hurts

This is the most misunderstood line of all, and it depends heavily on the technology you chose.

A site built on a content management system with extensions — WordPress being the obvious case — has software running on the server that receives constant updates. The core, the theme, the plugins. These updates aren't optional improvements: most of them fix security flaws that are already public at the moment the fix is released. A site that goes months without updates isn't frozen in time; it's becoming progressively more exposed.

The complication is that updating also breaks things. A plugin changes version and stops being compatible with the theme. A change in the core knocks a block out of alignment. None of this is dramatic, but it does require somebody to apply the update, check the site afterwards and know how to roll back if needed. That's human work, recurring, and that's what costs money — not clicking the button.

A static site, or one built with modern tooling and no exposed database, has a much smaller attack surface and very little security maintenance. In exchange, content changes can depend more on whoever built it, if it wasn't designed to be editable. Neither approach is free. You just need to know which of the two bills you're going to pay.

There's also a layer that doesn't depend on you at all: browsers change, versions of PHP or Node stop being supported, third-party APIs get discontinued. A site that works today can have a feature stop working eighteen months from now because an external service changed. You usually find out when a customer mentions that the contact form doesn't send.

## Security and monitoring

A small website isn't a target somebody picked out. It's a target found by automated scanning, which tests millions of addresses looking for known vulnerable versions. A neighbourhood clinic's website and a multinational's website appear on that list in exactly the same way.

The consequences are rarely the ones people imagine. It isn't the site going blank with a message from hackers: it's injected code redirecting a slice of your visits somewhere else, or pages created quietly that use your domain for spam. You find out late, usually when a search engine flags the site as unsafe, and the clean-up is always more expensive than prevention would have been.

What's actually needed is small and constant:

- **Automatic backups**, with several versions kept and, above all, tested. A backup that has never been restored is a hope, not a guarantee.
- **Uptime monitoring**, so you hear the site is down from a service rather than from a client.
- **Updates applied regularly**, for the reasons above.
- **Access control.** Accounts belonging to people who have left, passwords shared across the whole team, administrator access handed to someone who only needed to write an article.

## Small changes: the line nobody budgets for

This is the biggest source of friction between agencies and clients, and it's almost always a problem of expectation rather than of money.

A living website changes. The text on the services page changes because the offer changed. Two people join the team and three leave. There's a campaign that needs a landing page. Prices go up. A photograph no longer represents what the company does. None of this was in the brief, and all of it is normal.

What makes these changes expensive isn't the execution time. Swapping a piece of text takes minutes. The expensive part is everything around it: understanding the request, which usually arrives incomplete, finding where that thing lives, making the change, checking nothing broke on a phone, confirming with whoever asked, publishing. A request to «just change one sentence» is rarely one sentence and rarely stops at the first version.

There are three ways to handle this, and all of them are legitimate as long as you choose consciously.

**A retainer with hours included.** You pay a monthly amount that covers technical maintenance and a number of hours for changes. Predictable for both sides. It goes partly unused in the months when there's nothing to do, and that's precisely what buys you availability in the months when there is.

**Pay per request.** You ask, you get an estimate, you approve. Fair, but it creates friction: the cost of quoting half an hour of work is disproportionate, and the practical result is that small changes simply stop being made. The site degrades through the accumulation of outdated details.

**Autonomy.** The site is built so the internal team can edit text, images and simple pages without depending on anyone. It's the cheapest option over time and the one with the highest upfront cost, because it requires a well-built content system, components that don't break when someone writes twice the text they were designed for, and somebody trained to use it. When that last part is missing, the autonomy lasts about three weeks.

The right choice depends on who you have in-house. A company with a full-time marketing person should insist on autonomy. A six-person company with nobody in that role is better served by a retainer, even if it feels like paying for nothing in some months.

## Content and growth

There's one cost that isn't technical at all, and it's usually the largest: feeding the site. Articles, case studies, portfolio updates, new service pages. A site that receives no new content isn't penalised in any visible way — it just stops appearing, because other people are answering the same questions more recently and more completely.

This has a real cost: writing, editing, photographing, publishing. If the website was sold as a tool for winning work, that cost is part of how it operates, not an extra. A seven-page brochure site whose only job is to confirm the company exists can go years without adding anything. A site built to bring in work can't.

## The life cycle: three to five years

Maintenance doesn't prevent ageing. Even well cared for, a website reaches a point where the structure stops serving: the company has repositioned, the services don't fit the original architecture, the technology has too many patched-on layers, the design announces the year it was made.

The difference between the well-maintained site and the abandoned one isn't escaping that moment. It's arriving there in a condition to evolve rather than start over — with organised content, with traffic history worth something, with URLs that can be redirected instead of lost. A site abandoned for four years forces you to begin from zero, and that costs the price of a new website.

The most useful way to think about this is per year, not per project. Add up what the build cost, divide it by the number of years you expect it to last, then add what you'll spend each year keeping it running. That number is the true cost of having a website — and it's the number that should be on the table in the first meeting, not in the first unexpected invoice.
