People asking for a social media quote usually ask for the price of a post. It's an understandable question and almost always a useless one, because the same post can cost wildly different amounts depending on who decides what it says, how many versions of it exist, and who's going to make it again three weeks from now.

Social media design has a quality that sets it apart from a logo or a catalogue: it isn't a piece of work, it's a flow. The right question isn't what a post costs — it's what it costs to keep a coherent feed running for a year. And the answer depends, before anything else, on which pricing model you choose. There are three, they work in different ways, and each one has a scenario where it's clearly the cheapest.

## What you're actually paying for

Before the models, it's worth separating two things that often get quoted together and have almost nothing to do with each other.

The first is **deciding**: what this brand looks like on social, which typeface it uses, which grid, which palette, how it treats photography, how a carousel behaves compared with a single post, what happens when the text runs long. This gets done once, it takes time, it demands real skill, and it's the part that determines whether the feed reads as a brand or as a folder of files.

The second is **executing**: applying those decisions to new content, week after week. It's work that requires precision and very little invention, and its cost is almost entirely time multiplied by volume.

Nearly every pricing conversation that goes wrong goes wrong because someone is paying decision rates for execution work, or paying execution rates and expecting decisions. The three models below distribute those two components differently.

## Model 1 — Price per post

The easiest to understand and the easiest to get approved internally. You ask for one piece, you pay for one piece.

It works well in specific situations: a one-off campaign, an event announcement, a series of three posts for a product launch, a brand that publishes rarely and only when it genuinely has something to say. In those cases, paying per unit is honest on both sides and avoids committing budget to months where nothing is going to happen.

Where this model slips is on volume and consistency. Three problems show up every time.

- **Every piece starts from zero.** Without a defined system, each post means deciding the typography, the hierarchy and the image treatment all over again. That's decision time being paid for repeatedly — and the result, seen as a grid, is a feed with several accents.
- **Adaptations count as pieces.** The same content in square format, vertical for stories and horizontal for another platform can be quoted as three pieces or as one piece with two adaptations. The price difference is enormous, and this is where almost every misunderstanding happens.
- **Revisions have no natural limit.** If the number of rounds included isn't written down, the fourth version of a post comes out of somebody's pocket. Usually the designer's, once. The second time, it comes out of the next quote.

If you go with this model, insist that the quote states explicitly what counts as a unit, how many formats it includes and how many rounds of changes are covered. That isn't distrust — it's the only way to compare proposals.

## Model 2 — Monthly retainer

The most common model for brands that publish regularly. You pay a fixed amount each month and receive an agreed volume of pieces, usually against a calendar.

The real advantage isn't the bulk discount, though that exists. It's that the person designing stops getting to know the brand with every request. By the third month, a team working on a feed every week knows which images land, which tone won't survive approval, which formats perform and what legal is going to cut. That accumulated knowledge is why the fifth month costs less time than the first at the same price.

A retainer pays off when the cadence is steady and substantial, when more than one format is in play, and when the brand needs someone thinking about the whole rather than just today's post. It pays off above all when there's strategy involved: someone looking at the full month before the first file is opened.

It has its traps too, and they're worth knowing.

- **Unused volume.** In many contracts, what you don't use in a month is gone. That's legitimate — the team reserved the capacity — but it has to be clear from the start so nobody feels cheated in August.
- **The retainer turned into a request desk.** When a brand sends requests piecemeal as they occur to them, retainer time gets consumed in stops and starts, and very little is left for quality. The remedy is planning: deliver content in batches, in advance.
- **Confusion with social media management.** Designing posts is not the same thing as running social media. Writing the copy, scheduling, replying to comments, tracking metrics, managing paid campaigns — all of that is separate work with a separate price. If the quote doesn't distinguish them, someone is assuming the other party is covering it.

## Model 3 — Template system

Here the logic inverts. Instead of buying pieces, you buy the capacity to make them. You pay for a project, once, and what you get is a set of editable templates — typically in a tool the internal team can actually use — along with the rules for using them.

A well-built system isn't a folder with ten attractive files. It's a set of pieces designed to survive real use: what happens when the headline runs to two lines instead of one, what to do when the photograph is dark, where the logo goes when the image is already busy, which variant to use for a quote, for a statistic, for an announcement, for behind-the-scenes. It also includes the things you don't see: locked colours, installed typefaces, fixed grids, and a short list of what the team can change and what it can't.

This model pays off in three very recognisable scenarios.

**High volume of simple, repetitive content.** Brands publishing several times a week whose pieces follow three or four fixed patterns. Paying bespoke design rates to repeat the same pattern fifty times is waste.

**Content with urgency.** Anyone who needs to publish in the same hour the thing happens can't wait for a briefing and revision cycle. They need a mould and someone with their hands on the keyboard.

**Distributed teams.** Several shops, several offices, several countries, several partners. Without a system, everyone improvises and the brand dissolves. With a system, they improvise inside limits.

And it has an honest limit, which is worth saying out loud: a template system doesn't produce surprises. It handles the baseline work consistently and frees up budget so that the pieces which genuinely need to be special — a campaign, a launch, a date that matters — can be designed separately. A brand whose feed is made entirely of templates looks, after two years, exactly like that.

You should also budget for maintenance. Templates age, platforms change their formats, and needs appear that nobody anticipated. An annual review is to be expected and should be part of the sum.

## The variables that move the price most

Whichever model you pick, a handful of decisions shift any quote significantly. They're worth discussing before you ask for numbers, because they're what explains how two proposals for «social media design» can be radically different.

- **Number of formats per piece of content.** One idea declined across four proportions is adaptation work rather than creation — but it's still work.
- **Motion.** An animated piece costs several multiples of the same piece static. If motion is in the plan, it has to be quoted as its own line.
- **Where the images come from.** Original photography, paid stock, original illustration, or whatever the client has in a folder. Four scenarios with different costs and different outcomes, and the last is the most expensive in editing time.
- **Who writes the text.** If the copy arrives from elsewhere, the designer sets it. If the copy is expected from the designer, that's another skill and another price.
- **Rounds of revision.** Two rounds is the reasonable standard. Approval by committee is the single variable that inflates projects most, and it almost never appears in the brief.
- **Languages.** Each additional language multiplies the number of final files and introduces text-length problems that someone has to solve.

## The costs that don't appear in the proposal

There are three line items that rarely sit in a supplier's quote and are always paid by someone, usually in internal hours.

The first is **the brief**. Content delivered late, incomplete or in disorganised form turns production into archaeology. The time your team doesn't spend preparing the request is time the project spends twice over further down the line.

The second is **approval**. Someone has to look, decide and reply. If that person doesn't have the decision protected in their diary, the calendar slips and the cost shows up as work done in a rush.

The third is **publishing**. Exporting, uploading, checking how it looks on a phone, fixing whatever got cropped. Very little per piece, a great deal per year.

## How to decide

Work backwards, the way you would for any ongoing commitment. First, estimate the real volume — not what you'd like to publish, but what you'll manage to keep up in November. Then split that volume into two piles: what's repetitive and what's one-off.

If the repetitive pile is large, the conversation starts with a template system, with bespoke production reserved for the rest. If the one-off pile is large and the cadence is steady, the conversation starts with a retainer. If both piles are small and the activity is irregular, pay per piece and don't hire structure you don't need.

In practice, many brands end up with a combination: a base system built once, plus a smaller monthly engagement for the pieces the system doesn't solve. It usually comes out cheaper than either model on its own — and it's the only arrangement that solves the volume problem and the monotony problem at the same time.

Whichever route you take, the one thing not worth doing is buying pieces without buying a system. A feed without rules always costs more than it looks, and the price arrives later, in the form of a brand nobody recognises halfway down a scrolling screen.
