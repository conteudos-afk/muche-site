The short answer is two to four months for a complete identity for a small or medium-sized company, with a single brand and a reasonable set of applications. The useful answer is that the range tells you very little, because most of the time doesn't depend on whoever is designing — it depends on how fast decisions get made on your side.

It's worth understanding where the weeks go. Almost everyone assumes the bulk of the schedule sits in drawing the logo. It doesn't. Drawing is the most visible part and one of the fastest. What takes up calendar is deciding what the brand is before anyone opens a design application, and then turning an approved direction into a system other people can actually use.

## Phase 0: before the project starts

There's a period that never appears on a timeline and is entirely real: the time between first contact and signature.

Initial conversation, request for information, proposal, scope revision, internal approval, contract, deposit. In a company with a founder who decides alone, this can close in a week. In an organisation with a finance director and a procurement procedure, it can drag on for a month or more.

It isn't wasted time — it's time in which the scope should be clarified carefully, because that's where the schedule gets defined. But it is time. If you have a launch date in the diary, count backwards from it and include this phase.

## Phase 1: understanding and strategy

The longest phase and the only one that produces nothing to show. On a project with any depth, it easily takes up a third of the total schedule.

What happens here: interviews with the people who decide and the people who sell, conversations with real customers where possible, an audit of what already exists, analysis of what competitors do and say, definition of positioning, audience, attributes, verbal territory. Sometimes naming. Almost always a discussion about what the company is today versus what it would like to be in three years, which is rarely the same thing.

Two reasons this takes time.

The first is logistical. Booking six interviews with busy people isn't booking a meeting — it's fitting six diaries into two weeks. If one of the interviewees is a commercial director who's permanently travelling, it's three weeks.

The second is more uncomfortable: this is often the phase where internal disagreements surface that nobody knew existed. Two partners with different views of what the company sells. A sales team describing the business in terms the founder wouldn't recognise. Resolving that is branding work, and it's slow work, but it's better resolved now than discovered at the third concept presentation, when there's drawing on the table and everybody is arguing about colour because they can't argue about strategy.

Projects where the positioning is already clear and written down compress this phase into one or two weeks. Projects where it isn't can take six. This is where nearly all the variation between two apparently similar schedules lives.

## Phase 2: exploration and creative directions

Now the drawing starts. And it's faster than you'd think.

Exploring distinct territories, drawing, discarding, refining the two or three routes worth presenting, building each one far enough to see how it lives in application — this usually takes two to three weeks of concentrated work.

One detail changes the duration: presenting loose logos on a white background is quick and useless. Presenting each direction applied — on a shopfront, on packaging, on a phone screen, on an invoice — takes longer to prepare and saves weeks later, because it lets people decide with information instead of deciding on taste.

More directions doesn't mean more useful time. It means more time producing routes nobody believes in, and a harder decision at the end.

## Phase 3: presentation and decision

This phase takes however long your organisation takes. It's the most unpredictable of all and the one that destroys schedules most often.

A presentation lasts two hours. Deciding afterwards can take a day or five weeks. It depends on how many people have to weigh in, on whether they're all in the room, and on whether the decision criteria were agreed beforehand.

Three things shorten it significantly:

- **Everyone who decides is at the presentation.** If the partner who couldn't make it has to see it afterwards, there's a second presentation, delivered by someone who didn't prepare it, and a round of contradictions.
- **Feedback comes back consolidated, in one voice.** Five emails with scattered opinions aren't feedback: they're five incompatible requests that somebody will have to arbitrate.
- **The yardstick was set during the strategy phase.** If there's an approved positioning document, the conversation is «which of these directions serves what we wrote better». If there isn't, the conversation is about everyone's favourite colour.

It's worth booking the decision date on the same day you book the presentation date. Without that, the project sits still and nobody feels they're holding anything up.

## Phase 4: refinement

Once the direction is chosen, it gets tuned. Proportions, spacing, the letter that doesn't sit right, the reduced version that loses detail, the symbol that works at 200 mm and disappears at 18 px.

Normally two rounds, one to two weeks. It's meticulous and unspectacular work: at the next presentation, half the people won't see a difference. That's the point. If the difference were obvious, it wouldn't be refinement — it would be changing direction.

When a request for «what if we tried something completely different» turns up at this stage, the schedule goes back two phases. It happens, and sometimes it's the right call, but it needs to be clear that it's a restart and not an adjustment.

## Phase 5: building the system

The part clients underestimate almost every time.

With the logo settled, everything else has to be built: a full palette with values for screen, print, vinyl and embroidery; type hierarchy with sizes, weights and line heights; neutrals; functional colours; grids; iconography; photographic direction; composition principles. And then the applications, one by one.

This is where the inventory runs the schedule. Stationery, an email signature and a presentation template get resolved in two weeks. Menus, packaging, labels, interior signage, shopfronts, vehicles and uniforms are a month or two, because each piece has its own constraints and many require talking to suppliers before they can be designed at all.

Make the complete list at the start. An application that appears halfway through isn't an unforeseen event, it's a gap in the brief — and it adds weeks that weren't planned anywhere.

## Phase 6: manual and handover

One to two weeks, and it's time well spent.

Documentation of the rules, files organised in every format, versions named so it's obvious which one is current, an essentials sheet for people who only need three pieces of information a day. It isn't admin: it's the difference between an identity that still holds together after two years and one that falls apart the first time a supplier opens the wrong file.

It's also the phase most often cut when a project is already running late. Cutting here postpones the cost, it doesn't remove it.

## What runs in parallel and has its own timeline

Two things worth keeping outside the project schedule.

**Trademark registration.** At the national office or at EUIPO, it has its own timelines, measured in months, including opposition periods. If the name is new, the prior-rights search should be done early — preferably before the design is settled — because discovering a clash after the shopfront is installed is expensive and slow to fix.

**Production.** Printing stationery, manufacturing signage, producing packaging, applying vinyl. None of this is in the design schedule and all of it has supplier lead times, colour proofs and the occasional reprint. An identity delivered in March is not a visible brand in March.

And the website, almost always. Treating it as a natural extension of the manual is the most common way a launch date blows up.

## What actually delays projects

In order of frequency, and none of these causes is the drawing.

- **Slow or contradictory feedback.** A week of waiting, repeated four times, is a month.
- **Decision-makers who appear late.** Someone with veto power joining at the fourth meeting forces the whole conversation to be had again.
- **Information that doesn't arrive.** Content, product lists, contact details, data for the applications. Design sits waiting and nobody logs that as a delay.
- **Unresolved strategy that comes back disguised as an opinion about colour.** The most reliable symptom that phase 1 was left half done.
- **Scope that grows along the way.** More applications, another language, a sub-brand that turns out to exist after all.

## What can be compressed, and what can't

If you have a fixed date — a trade fair, an opening, an investment round — there are honest ways to shorten things.

Reduce the number of applications at handover. Do the essentials now and the rest in phases. Reduce the number of directions presented, as long as the alignment phase is stronger. Get the decision-makers into a single session and fix the feedback dates in the diary at kick-off. Start talking to suppliers while the system is still being built.

What isn't worth compressing is the understanding phase. A project that skips strategy looks fast for three weeks and then stalls at the decision, because there's no criterion for choosing between two equally competent proposals. The time saved at the start gets spent twice over in the middle, and usually with more friction.

It's also not worth cutting the manual or the system versions. Those two things don't show at handover — they show six months later, when the brand is being applied by people who were never in a project meeting.

## How to read a schedule in a proposal

Look for three things. If they're there, the timeline is probably real.

- **Decision dates marked, not just presentation dates.** A timeline that only shows deliverables from the studio's side is an incomplete timeline.
- **The number of revision rounds.** «Unlimited revisions» isn't generosity: it's a project with no predictable end.
- **What happens if feedback is late.** A serious timeline says the schedule slips by the same amount. If it doesn't say so now, it will get said later, in a worse conversation.

A visual identity doesn't take as long as the designer needs to draw. It takes as long as the company needs to make up its mind, plus the time the drawing needs to become a system. Organise the first part and you buy weeks for the second.
