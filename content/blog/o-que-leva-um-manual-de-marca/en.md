A brand guidelines document doesn't exist to look good. It exists so that someone who has never spoken to you can do something with your brand and get it right. An intern building a deck, a vinyl supplier cutting a shopfront, a developer building a form, an agency that joins the project two years from now. If those people can move forward without phoning you, the document works. If they can't, it's a PDF.

That's the test, and it's the only one. Most guidelines fail it — not because they're bad, but because they were written with the presentation meeting in mind rather than the day someone actually needs them.

## The two documents that get confused

There are two distinct things that often end up glued together in the same file.

One is the **identity presentation**: the document that explains the thinking, shows the before and after, displays applications in mockups, justifies the direction. It exists to persuade, to align the people making the decision, to get the project signed off. That's legitimate and useful — but it's a single-use document.

The other is the **usage manual**: the document someone opens in a hurry, looking for one specific answer, probably on one single page. It doesn't need to convince anybody. It needs to be consultable.

When both live inside the same ninety-page file, the usage manual gets buried. The person who needs to know the minimum clear space around the symbol has to get past sixteen pages of moodboard and three of brand narrative. After two attempts, they stop opening the document and start guessing.

The most useful decision you can make is to separate the two. The presentation goes into the project archive. The manual stays short, organised by task, and lives where people actually work.

## Logo: the part everyone thinks about and few resolve

This is the section that never goes missing and that, even so, is usually missing the essentials.

What you genuinely need:

- **Which versions exist and what each one is for.** Horizontal, vertical, symbol on its own, reduced version. Showing them side by side isn't enough: it has to be written down when each one gets used. «Symbol alone only above 40 px and only when the brand name is already present in the context» is an instruction. Four unlabelled lockups is not.
- **Minimum size.** In millimetres for print and in pixels for screen. It's the most ignored instruction and the one that causes the most damage, because a logo with fine detail reduced to 18 px turns into a smudge and nobody notices until it's printed.
- **Clear space.** Defined using a unit taken from the logo itself — the height of a letter, half the symbol — rather than in centimetres, so it scales on its own.
- **The files, and which one to use.** SVG for web, EPS or vector PDF for suppliers, PNG with transparent background for people who can only open a PNG. With the real file names written into the document. If nobody knows which of the eleven files in the folder is the right one, the decision goes back to whoever has their hand on the mouse.
- **Behaviour on backgrounds.** On the brand colour, on black, on photography. And what to do when the photograph is light on one side and dark on the other — which always happens.

What tends to be padding: the «concept» page showing the symbol overlaid with circles and golden lines, demonstrating that everything was built on a harmonic grid. It's pleasant to look at in the presentation and nobody has ever used it to do any actual work.

The misuse section is a case of its own. Showing the logo stretched, rotated and drop-shadowed is predictable and almost useless — nobody does that on purpose. What's worth listing are the errors that are likely for that specific brand: the old version still circulating, the symbol redrawn by a supplier because «the file wouldn't open», the logo placed on the secondary colour where it disappears.

## Colour: where the same things are always missing

Almost every set of guidelines carries two primary colours with codes. Almost none carries the rest, and it's the rest that people need.

The essentials:

- **Values by substrate.** Hex, RGB, CMYK and Pantone. And, where possible, the vinyl reference and the thread colour for embroidery, because those suppliers don't work with Pantone.
- **Defined neutrals.** The greys and off-whites cover most of the surface of a website or a package. If they're not in the document, someone will use whatever default grey the tool in front of them offers.
- **Functional colours.** Success, error, warning. Not defining them means inheriting them from a code library.
- **Proportion of use.** Which colour dominates, which supports, which appears in small doses. A palette without proportions lets you build two completely different brands from the same five colours.
- **Pairs with enough contrast to read.** The brand colour works well for buttons and badly for body copy. The document has to say which combinations are allowed in text.

Padding: the pages with colour blocks arranged in abstract compositions, with poetic names given to each shade. The name is only useful if it's the name the team actually says out loud — otherwise it's one more layer of translation between the person and the hex code they need.

## Typography: rules, not specimens

The typography section in most guidelines is a pangram in three weights and a full alphabet set at a large size. It's handsome and it helps nobody lay out a page.

What does help:

- **The hierarchy, defined.** Main heading, subheadings, body, captions, buttons. With size, weight, line height and letter spacing. A small, tight scale is more useful than a list of possibilities.
- **Fallback fonts.** What gets used when the licensed typeface isn't available: in email, in shared documents, in decks made by people who don't have the family installed. Without this, the alternative gets picked at random and the brand ends up with two faces.
- **The state of the licence.** How many seats it covers, whether web is included, whether app use is included, who holds it. It's dull information and it's the kind that prevents real problems.
- **Composition rules.** Alignment, use of capitals, whether headings take final punctuation, maximum line length for text.

Padding: the history of the typeface and the name of the type designer. Interesting, irrelevant to someone laying out a leaflet at five in the afternoon.

## Photography and imagery: the worst-handled section

It usually comes down to a grid of reference images with no captions. Whoever looks at it gets the mood and doesn't get the rule.

What needs to be written down, in words:

- **What kind of light.** Hard or diffuse, directional or frontal, warm or neutral.
- **How people appear in the images.** Looking at the camera or not, posed or in action, in what kind of spaces.
- **What never appears.** This is almost always more useful than the list of what's allowed. No white studio backgrounds, no stock images of people smiling in meetings, no heavy saturation filters.
- **Framing and ratio.** The formats the brand uses and how much margin it leaves.
- **Treatment.** Contrast level, whether there's a colour cast, limits on editing.

A practical way to assess this section: hand it to a photographer who doesn't know the project and ask what they'd do on a shoot. If they can describe the shoot, the section is finished. If all they can say is «I get the mood», everything is still missing.

## Tone of voice: examples, not adjectives

A list of three adjectives — «warm, confident, rigorous» — doesn't change a single sentence anyone writes. Everybody already thinks they write like that.

What changes behaviour is pairs. The same message written two ways, one right and one wrong, side by side. An email subject line, an error message, a social caption, a reply to a complaint. Five or six well-chosen pairs teach more than three pages of principles.

It's also worth including the mechanical decisions nobody knows where to look up: how formal the address is, whether emoji are used, how the brand name is written in running text, whether there are internal capitals, how prices and dates are formatted. They're details, and inconsistency in them is visible.

## Applications: the real ones, not the mockups

Almost every set of guidelines has mockups of paper bags and building façades. Few brands are ever going to produce a paper bag.

The applications section should show what the brand produces every week: the email signature, the presentation template, the proposal template, the social post cover, the invoice, the business card if anybody still prints them. With editable files, not just images. A picture of a beautiful email signature solves nothing; a block of HTML you can copy and paste solves it.

And then there's the digital part, which gets systematically left out: button states, form fields, border weights, corner radii, grid and breakpoints. If they're not in the document, they're in one person's head — and brands can't be kept in one person's head.

## Format matters almost as much as content

A seventy-page PDF gets consulted for the first few weeks and then never again. It's worth thinking in three layers.

An **essentials sheet**, one or two pages, with what gets looked up every day: logos and files, colour codes, type hierarchy, three unbreakable rules. This is the one most people will use.

A **full manual**, organised by task rather than by discipline — «making a presentation», «preparing files for a supplier» — with a navigable, searchable index.

And a **file folder** with clear names, versioned, where the current version is distinguishable from the old ones without effort. A large share of application errors doesn't come from ignorance of the rules: it comes from someone having opened the wrong file.

## What the document has to say about itself

Two things, and they take five minutes to write.

**Who decides what isn't covered.** There will always be a new case and a supplier saying they can't do it exactly that way. If there's no name to ask, the decision gets made by whoever is in the biggest hurry.

**The date and the version.** A document with no date sits on equal footing with the 2019 version still circulating by email, and nobody knows which is which.

A good set of brand guidelines is short, specific and dull to read. That isn't a flaw: it's the sign that it was written to be used rather than to be presented.
