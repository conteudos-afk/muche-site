The most common reason brand podcasts fail isn't poor production or weak content. It's the absence of strategy. They launch without a clear answer to who they're for, what they're meant to achieve, and how they'll keep going past the first three episodes. Those are questions that have to be answered before anyone switches on a microphone.

This is hard to accept because production is the visible part. It's where the equipment is, the treated room, the editing, the theme music. It's also the part money can solve. Strategic decisions can't be solved with money and don't show up in the finished file — but they're the ones that determine whether there's a finished file at episode thirty.

## Audience before topic

Most podcasts define themselves by topic: a show about marketing, a show about entrepreneurship, a show about wellbeing. The better approach is to define the audience first, and only then find the angle on the topic that's most useful and most distinctive for that specific group of people.

The difference isn't rhetorical. «A podcast about architecture» excludes nobody and therefore calls to nobody. «Conversations with people restoring an old building for the first time» excludes almost everyone, and because of that, the people who recognise themselves in it don't let go. The more precisely you can describe your listener, the more precisely you can serve them — and the easier everything else becomes, from choosing guests to writing each episode title.

There's a simple test for whether you've actually defined an audience. Can you name three real people, who exist, who would listen to this week's episode? Not demographic profiles — people. If the answer is no, you still have a topic and you don't yet have a show.

### The angle is what separates you

Once the audience is defined, what's left is finding what nobody is giving them. Usually it isn't a new topic: it's a perspective, a level of depth, or a kind of guest who doesn't turn up anywhere else.

It's worth listening to three or four shows your likely audience already follows and writing down, for each one, what they do well and what they avoid. Business podcasts avoid talking about concrete numbers. Creative podcasts avoid talking about what went wrong. Corporate podcasts avoid guests who might disagree. Those avoidances are the space where a new show fits — if you have the stomach to occupy it.

### What it's actually for

The other question to answer before recording is what the podcast does for the brand. Awareness within a niche is one goal. Building relationships with people who otherwise wouldn't take your call is another — and for many companies it's the most profitable one, because an invitation to an hour-long conversation opens doors a sales proposal never will. Feeding the recruitment pipeline is another. Selling directly isn't, or at least isn't realistic over any short timeframe.

Decide this first, because it changes the production. A relationship podcast doesn't need many listeners: it needs the right listeners and well-chosen guests. An awareness podcast needs volume and episodes that stand up in isolation, because most people will arrive through a single loose episode rather than from the beginning.

## Format is a commitment

Interview shows, solo narrative, roundtable, documentary — each format carries different production demands, different audience expectations and different creative possibilities. The format you choose is a commitment you'll live with for as long as the show runs.

It's worth looking at the real cost of each one, which is rarely what it looks like from outside.

- **Interview.** Looks like the easy format and is the most demanding in invisible work. Every episode means identifying the guest, persuading them, scheduling, preparing questions they haven't already been asked everywhere else, and then editing a conversation that rarely runs in a straight line. The quality of the show ends up depending on other people's diaries, which is the variable you control least.
- **Solo narrative.** You don't have to persuade anyone, you record when you want and you control the result completely. In exchange, you write everything. A well-written twenty-minute episode is a long piece of text, revised, read aloud until it sounds natural. It's the format that depends most on you actually having something to say.
- **Roundtable with your own team.** Cheap to schedule, expensive to coordinate — getting three people from the same company in a room regularly is harder than it sounds. And it carries a specific risk: internal conversations tend to slide into private jokes and references nobody outside can follow.
- **Documentary or narrative series.** The format that produces the best result and the one almost nobody can sustain. It demands research, several recorded sources, sound design, slow assembly. It works well as a closed series of six or eight episodes; it nearly always fails when someone tries to turn it into an ongoing show.

Choose the format based on what you can sustain and what best serves the content, not on what sounds most impressive in a meeting. A good way to test it is to do the arithmetic for a normal week: how many hours, out of your real diary rather than your ideal one, does this format require. Multiply by fifty.

### Decide the length and the structure too

Format isn't only genre. It's the typical length, the internal structure and what repeats from episode to episode. A show with a recognisable opening, a fixed segment and a predictable close is easier to listen to and enormously easier to produce, because you stop designing each episode from scratch.

On length: it should be whatever the content needs, not whatever sounds respectable. Ninety-minute episodes that could have been forty minutes are the most common way to lose listeners without understanding why, because people don't unsubscribe — they simply stop finishing episodes, and then stop starting them.

## Cadence sets expectations

Weekly, fortnightly, monthly — whatever cadence you commit to, your audience will internalise it. Breaking that cadence without warning erodes trust. Before you launch, be honest about your capacity to produce episodes consistently over time.

A fortnightly show sustained for two years is worth more than a weekly show that goes quiet after six months. And silence carries a cost that isn't easily recovered: someone who deleted the app or stopped seeing the show in their list doesn't come back on their own initiative.

Two practices prevent almost every break.

**Batch recording.** Booking four conversations in a single day or week and keeping those episodes in reserve. A show that goes live with three finished episodes in the drawer survives an illness, a holiday and one bad month of client work. A show that records on Monday what it publishes on Wednesday lives permanently on the edge of failure.

**Thinking in seasons.** Eight or ten episodes, an announced break, a new season. It's more honest than an implied promise of eternity, and it gives you a natural moment to assess what worked, change format, or stop without that being a public failure. Announced breaks erode no trust at all; silent breaks erode all of it.

## The first ten episodes are the hardest

Podcast growth is slow and non-linear. Most shows that eventually succeed didn't look successful in the first three months. In the early episodes you'll have few listeners, you'll hear yourself back and dislike it, and you'll be tempted to conclude that the idea was wrong.

The idea might be wrong, but the first episodes aren't evidence of that. They're the period in which you learn to carry a conversation without interrupting, to cut the question that goes nowhere, to notice that the best part was at minute forty and should have opened the episode. Nobody does this well by the tenth recording, let alone the first.

### What to measure while there are no numbers to celebrate

Downloads in the first few months tell you very little. Three other things are worth more. First: do people finish the episodes? A small audience that listens to the end is a show that works. Second: do some episodes clearly stand out from the rest? That tells you more about the right angle than any amount of planning beforehand. Third, the least quantifiable and the most useful: has anyone mentioned the show to you without you raising it?

### The right horizon

The brands that succeed with podcasting are the ones that commit to a window long enough to find their voice, build their audience and refine their format. Twelve months is a reasonable minimum. It's at two years that the real returns start to compound — when there's an archive that has value on its own, when guests say yes without needing an explanation, and when people arrive through recommendation rather than campaign.

This has a practical implication that often gets ignored: the commitment has to be secured before you start. If the budget is annual and will be reviewed at month six on the basis of numbers, the show will be cancelled at month six, because that's precisely when the numbers look worst. Better to decide a minimum horizon up front and defend it internally than to launch with enthusiasm and die at the first cost review.

## Before you switch on the microphone

A podcast is a significant investment of time and creative energy. Approached strategically, it's one of the most powerful brand-building tools available: nobody gives forty minutes of continuous attention to a brand in any other format. Approached without strategy, it's an expensive experiment that produces three episodes and a lesson learned the hard way.

If you can write down on one page who the listener is, what angle is useful to them, what format you can sustain, at what cadence and for how long, you have a show. If you can't, you have a desire to make a podcast — and that, on its own, has never reached episode ten.
