«What does a day of filming cost?» is a reasonable question built on a faulty assumption: that a day is a stable unit, like a square metre or an hour of a solicitor's time. It isn't. A shoot day is a package somebody assembled — this many people, this much kit, this many kilometres, this many hours — and the price is the sum of that package.

Which is why there are shoot days that cost a few hundred pounds and shoot days that cost tens of thousands, and both are called the same thing. When you receive two quotes that are miles apart for what looks to you like the same job, what you're holding isn't two prices for the same thing. It's two different versions of the day.

This article won't give you a number. It gives you the parts, so you can look at a proposal and understand what's actually in it.

## First part: the people

This is where the biggest slice lives, and it's the part that varies most between proposals.

Every role on a set exists because somebody, somewhere, found out what happens without it. On a small production, several roles collapse into one person. On a large one, they separate. There's no virtue in having a big crew — there are consequences to having a small one, and that's what you have to weigh.

- **Director.** Who decides what gets shot, in what order, and when it's good enough. On a short crew, that's the same person standing behind the camera, and it shows: whoever is framing isn't watching the person speaking.
- **Director of photography.** Who designs the light and the image. It's the role that makes the biggest difference to how finished the thing feels, and the one you can least improvise on the day.
- **Camera operator.** May or may not be the same person as the DOP. On anything with movement, it rarely is.
- **Camera assistant.** Focus, lenses, cards, batteries. Looks like a luxury until you have an entire interview two centimetres out of focus.
- **Sound.** Microphones, levels, background noise. Sound isn't fixed afterwards — it's reduced, with a loss. A day with several people speaking and nobody dedicated to sound is a day with the risk concentrated in exactly the wrong place.
- **Sparks and grips.** Who rigs the lights, runs cable, carries kit. On small productions that's everyone, and everyone takes longer.
- **Production on location.** Who solves everything that isn't creative: access, timings, people arriving late, lunch, the security guard nobody told. Without this role the day slows down and the person who should be thinking about the image is on the phone.
- **Hair, makeup and wardrobe.** They come in when there are people on camera who have to look right for hours under hard light.

To all of this you add something that rarely gets explained: a crew's day isn't the hours the camera is recording. It's the hours of setup before, the derig after, and the travel. Four useful hours of filming is easily a full day for the people standing there. Which is why half a day rarely costs half.

And there are days nobody films and everybody still pays for: the DOP's prep day, the day for collecting and testing kit, the day it goes back. In a serious proposal, either they're itemised or they're folded into the day rate.

## Second part: the equipment

This is the part everybody imagines is the biggest, and it almost never is. Even so, it varies a lot, and it's worth knowing why.

The camera body is the least decisive line in the kit budget. Lenses usually cost more than the camera, and they define a large share of how the image looks. Then comes lighting, which is where the cost grows without anyone noticing: fixtures, diffusion, flags, stands, generators if you're away from a building, batteries for everything.

Three things move the kit figure on a given day:

- **How many points of light you need to control.** Filming one person in a room with a good window is one thing. Filming a windowless industrial hall at night is another order of magnitude — and almost all of the difference is lighting, and the people rigging it.
- **Whether there's movement.** Slider, motorised head, crane, gimbal, dolly. Each of these adds hire cost and adds setup time, which is the expensive part.
- **Whether there's a second camera.** Two cameras save time in an interview and give the edit options. But they demand more lenses, more cards, someone else to operate, and more material to sync afterwards.

Redundancy counts too. A production company that brings one camera and nothing else is betting that nothing breaks. One that brings backup kit is charging you for something it will probably never use — and that cost is the difference between a day that goes badly and a day that's lost.

## Third part: the location

Location is the most underestimated part, because it doesn't look like it has a price. It does, and sometimes it's higher than the equipment.

Filming in the client's office, on a weekday, with parking outside and a lift, is the cheap scenario. Everything that moves away from that adds.

- **Distance.** Travel for crew and kit, fuel, tolls. Past a certain distance there's accommodation and evening meals, and the day starts occupying a day and a half of calendar.
- **Number of locations in one day.** Moving costs two to three hours between derigging, loading, travelling, unloading and rigging again. Two locations in a day is possible; four is a plan pretending to be a plan.
- **Access.** No lift, stairs, loading a hundred metres from the door: all of that is crew time, and crew time is money.
- **Hiring the space.** Studios, hotels, restaurants, private venues. Many charge by the hour and count the setup hours.
- **Permits.** Public streets, council property, shopping centres, transport networks. There are fees, lead times and sometimes a requirement for specific insurance.
- **Conditions.** Noise that forces you to stop and wait, changing light, wind, heat inside a factory. None of this appears on the invoice under its own name — it appears as fewer shots completed in the same hours.

There's one more variable that doesn't show up in the quote and gets paid anyway: the client company's time. A production line stopped, a shop closed, ten staff pulled off their work for a morning. In the production company's accounts that's zero. In yours, it isn't.

## Fourth part: everything that produces no image at all

Public liability and equipment insurance. Catering, which isn't a luxury — it's the difference between a crew that holds up for ten hours and one that gives out at seven. Drives to offload the material on site, duplicated, because a lost card is the entire shoot. Image releases. Transport for the kit.

In a proposal where this layer doesn't exist, either the job really is one person in a room, or somebody forgot it and it will reappear later under a different name.

## Why two quotes for the same day can look so different

It's almost never margin. It's scope, and scope hides in details you don't catch on a first read.

**Number of people.** This is the single biggest source of difference. Two names against six names is a different budget and a different day. The cheaper proposal isn't wrong — it's telling you that sound, lighting and production will be handled by someone who already has something else in their hands.

**The real length of the day.** Ten hours on location isn't the same as eight. Nor is it the same as eight with billable overtime from the ninth hour. It's worth asking what happens when the day overruns, because it nearly always does.

**What's included before and after.** Technical recce, prep day, offload and backup, delivery of the rushes. A proposal that includes all of that looks more expensive than one that quotes only the day and invoices the rest later.

**Kit level.** A small lighting package or a full one, different grades of lens, with or without backup.

**Who carries the risk.** Rain, breakdown, postponement. If it isn't written down who pays for a day cancelled the night before, the implication is that you do.

**Whether post-production is inside.** There are proposals where «shoot day» includes the edit and others where it includes none of it. Comparing the two on day rate is comparing different things.

## What to ask before you compare

With these answers, two distant quotes stop being a mystery:

- How many people are on location and in what roles, name by name?
- How many hours of attendance are planned, and what does each extra hour cost?
- What does the kit package include, in lighting and in lenses?
- Is the technical recce included?
- Who handles permits and who pays the fees?
- Are travel, accommodation and meals in or out?
- What happens if the day is postponed with 24 hours' notice?
- Who owns the raw material and when do you get it?

## How to bring the cost of a day down without ruining it

The most effective approach isn't negotiating the day rate. It's reducing what you ask the day to do.

Concentrating locations saves whole hours of travel and rigging. Reducing the number of people who speak on camera shortens everything: fewer lighting setups to rebuild, less warming-up time, less material to review in the edit. Choosing a space with decent natural light saves on equipment and on the people rigging it. Preparing properly means the hours of the day get spent filming rather than deciding.

The cut that always ends up expensive is the opposite one: keeping the ambition of the plan and taking people off the crew. The same number of scenes remain for fewer hands, and what gives way first is what's least visible on the day and most visible afterwards — the sound, the second take, the coverage.

A shoot day is a closed window. Inside it, what determines the price is how many people are there, with what kit, in what place, and for how long. Any quote that doesn't let you see those four things isn't being simpler. It's just being less clear.
