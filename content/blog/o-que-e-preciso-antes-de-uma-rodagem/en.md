On a shoot, the client's share of the work is almost always underestimated. There's an assumption that the production company handles everything and the client turns up on the day to watch. Partly true: the crew, the kit, the logistics and the plan for the day belong to whoever is producing. But there's a set of decisions only the client can make, and none of them can be made on the day.

This is the list of those decisions. It isn't long. The problem is that each one, left open, produces a concrete and entirely predictable consequence on the shoot day — and it's worth knowing what that consequence is before you live through it.

## An approved script, approved by whoever decides

The first item isn't «have a script». It's having a script approved by the person with the final word. Those are different things, and the distance between them costs money.

A script approved by the marketing department and not yet seen by the managing director is not an approved script. It's a draft wearing the clothes of a finished document. If the person who decides only reads it the night before, one of two things happens: either you shoot anyway and discover at the end that the video doesn't do the job, or it gets rewritten overnight and the crew arrives to a day whose plan no longer matches what was prepared.

What depends on the script is far more than the text. The shot list comes out of it. The number of hours comes out of the shot list. The locations come out of the scenes. The people who speak come out of the ideas that have to be said. When a script changes substantially, everything downstream changes with it — which is why the script lock date needs to sit several days before the shoot, not one.

Locking a script means three practical things:

- **It's written.** Not a set of bullet points, not a conversation about what would be nice to show. Actual text, or a sequence of concrete actions, or an ordered set of questions.
- **It's been read aloud and timed**, if there's narration. Text that looks the right length on the page usually runs thirty seconds longer when spoken.
- **It has the sign-off of whoever decides.** One person. If it's three, the shoot will carry three versions of what matters and will end up filming all three.

### What happens on the day when it's missing

People argue. It's the most expensive way to lose a morning. The crew is set up, the lighting is done, and two people on the client side are resolving, in front of everybody, a question that should have been settled in a meeting a fortnight earlier. What comes out of that is never the best version of the idea. It's the version both of them accepted so the day could continue.

## Locations confirmed, visited and actually free

A confirmed location is somewhere a person has physically stood, at the time of day you'll be filming, with written permission from whoever controls it, and with an assurance that it's free on that date.

Remove any one of those four and it isn't confirmed.

The time of the recce matters more than it seems. A room with magnificent window light at five in the afternoon can be a grey box at ten in the morning, which is when the shoot will be standing in it. A street that's empty on Sunday has deliveries on Tuesday morning. Visiting at the wrong hour is nearly as risky as not visiting at all.

Availability is the client's side of things too. The client is the one who knows whether the meeting room is booked, whether the production line stops or doesn't, whether there's building work on the floor above, whether it's stocktaking day, whether reception will be full of people from an event. The production company can ask — but it only asks what occurs to it to ask, and your internal calendar doesn't occur to anyone outside it.

### What happens on the day when it's missing

The common scenario isn't being locked out. It's getting in and finding the space occupied by something else. So you shoot in an alternative spot chosen in five minutes, and the video ends up with one scene that visibly wasn't planned: the wrong wall, a background with no relationship to the brand, a tight frame hiding whatever sits beside it.

The second common scenario is noise. Roadworks, a compressor, a ventilation system that can't be switched off. Bad sound isn't repaired in post — it's reduced, with a loss in quality. If the most important sentence in the video has a lorry over it, the sentence doesn't exist.

## Permissions, all of them, in writing

Permissions come in three types, and it helps to handle them separately, because they fail for different reasons.

**Permission for the space.** Who has the authority to say you can film there? In your own office, easy. In a shared building, it's the building management. In a shopping centre it's the centre's administration, with notice and often with insurance. On a public street it depends on the local council and the scale of what you're doing — a camera on the shoulder in a busy street is one thing; closing half a pavement with tripods, a dolly and a production van is another, and that needs a permit.

**Image releases from the people on camera.** Everybody identifiable in frame signs. Staff included — the fact that somebody works for the company doesn't give the company the right to use their face in a campaign. And the document should state the purposes, the media and the duration, because a video that was meant for the website always ends up as an advert.

**Permission for third parties who appear without being invited.** Customers in a shop, patients in a clinic, children in a school. In a closed space that's handled with signage at the entrance and careful production management. With minors there's no shortcut: written consent from the legal guardian, signed, in advance.

### What happens on the day when it's missing

There are two versions. The first is somebody turning up mid-morning and stopping the shoot — most common in shared buildings, and it costs the whole day.

The second is worse because it's silent. Everything is filmed, everything is cut, the video is finished, and only then does somebody think to ask whether that person signed. If they didn't, and they've since left the company or fallen out with it, the video has to be re-edited without them — and sometimes they're the one saying the line that holds the rest together.

## People, properly briefed

The part of the list that depends most on the client, and fails most often, is this one: the company's own people who will appear on camera.

Briefing properly is not sending a calendar invite titled «filming». It's telling each person what they'll be asked to do, how long it takes, what they'll be talking about and what to wear. Someone who arrives not knowing they were going to speak takes forty minutes to relax — and forty minutes of a shoot morning is an entire scene.

Worth deciding in advance, in writing:

- **Who speaks and about what.** Three people saying the same thing is the natural outcome of nobody dividing the topics. Only one survives the edit.
- **What time each person arrives.** People on camera don't wait three hours well. They get tense, or they leave because they had a meeting.
- **What they wear.** Fine stripes and small checks create patterns in the image. Other brands' logos have to be hidden. A simple instruction — plain colours, no pattern, a second option in the bag — handles it.
- **The job title in the caption.** Confirm it beforehand. Finding out at the end that the title changed means redoing graphics and a fresh round of approval.
- **A substitute.** If the person who says the central line of the video has something come up, who says it?

There's one more decision that belongs only to the client: who, on the day, decides on behalf of the company. It has to be someone with authority and with nothing else scheduled. A shoot generates a dozen small questions an hour, and all of them need an answer immediately.

### What happens on the day when it's missing

The shoot slows to the speed of the hardest person to track down. The crew waits, the DOP rebuilds a lighting setup that was already built, and the shot list starts shrinking. The first thing cut is always coverage — the detail shots, the atmosphere, the gestures. Which is exactly what the edit needs to hide a cut, shorten a long answer or open the video.

## Physical access and the things nobody writes down

There's always a practical layer left over, and it's almost entirely on the client's side.

- **Entry and unloading.** What time the building opens, where the van parks, whether there's a lift or three flights of stairs with fifteen cases.
- **Security and reception.** Crew names submitted in advance, if the building requires it. A crew stuck in reception at eight in the morning is half an hour lost in the best light of the day.
- **Power.** Where the sockets are and who tells the maintenance team.
- **A base room.** Somewhere to put kit down, change clothes, do makeup, leave bags.
- **Who switches off the noise.** Air conditioning, background music, the door buzzer, the tannoy. There's always someone responsible, and sometimes they aren't in the building.
- **Products, samples, equipment.** If the video shows the product, the product has to be there, clean, in the right quantity and in the current version. It's remarkable how many product shoots begin with somebody going to fetch the product.

## The minimum list

If you want to reduce all of this to something you can confirm a week out:

- Script locked and signed off by whoever decides.
- Locations visited at the right hour, confirmed and free on the day.
- Space permissions and image releases signed.
- People briefed, with topic, time, clothing and a substitute.
- One person from the client with authority and free all day.
- Access, power, base room and noise sorted.
- Products and materials on site.
- A rain plan, if there are exteriors.

None of these points requires technical skill. They require decisions and phone calls, which is exactly why they get postponed: they don't feel urgent while the date is still far away.

A shoot day isn't a normal working day with cameras in it. It's a closed window, at a fixed cost, to gather raw material you can't go back for without paying the whole thing again. Whatever isn't decided beforehand will be decided there — quickly, by whoever is standing closest, and almost never in the best way.
