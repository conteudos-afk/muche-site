Typography is one of the most underestimated brand decisions a company makes. Most businesses choose a typeface based on what looks good on a screen, in a presentation, with three words at large size and plenty of space around them. Very few ask the deeper question: what does this typeface say about us? Every typeface carries a personality, a history and a set of associations — and whether you intended it or not, those associations transfer to your brand.

It's worth understanding why that happens. A typeface was drawn at a particular moment, by a particular person, for a particular purpose. It was then used for decades in certain contexts and not in others. The person looking at it doesn't know that history, but they recognise the context: they've seen similar shapes on contracts, on festival posters, on pharmacy packaging, in school textbooks. The recognition is unconscious and it's fast — it arrives before the words get read.

## Serif, sans-serif and what they communicate

Serif typefaces — the ones with small strokes at the ends of the letterforms — tend to communicate tradition, authority and refinement. Sans-serifs communicate modernity, clarity and directness. Neither is better than the other; the question is which one serves your positioning. A luxury brand using a geometric sans is making a deliberate statement about contemporary elegance. A brand with history using a modern grotesque is saying it has moved on.

But splitting type into two families is far too crude to decide anything with. Within each one there are differences of register much larger than the difference between the two.

**Among serifs.** A humanist serif, with strokes that still recall the hand and the pen, reads as editorial, literate, comfortable — it's the register of a well-set book. A high-contrast serif of the didone kind, with thick stems and hairline serifs, reads as fashion, perfume, magazine cover: elegant and, at the same time, cold. A slab serif, with heavy rectangular serifs, reads as sturdy, industrial, American, the sort of thing you use and don't break. Three different worlds, all sitting under the same word.

**Among sans-serifs.** A neutral grotesque doesn't say anything in particular, and that's its job: it lets the message speak and doesn't compete. A geometric, built from circles and straight lines, reads as project work, architecture, the optimism of a certain kind of modernism — and it comes with a practical problem attached to the beauty: too many circles make long words less legible at small sizes. A humanist sans, with open shapes and proportions inherited from handwriting, is the easiest to read on screen and the most discreet of the lot; it's why so many interfaces are built on them.

The useful question isn't «serif or sans». It's this: when somebody sees this for half a second, what kind of company do they think we are? If the answer isn't the one you wanted, colour and the logo won't fix it.

## The associations you didn't choose

Some typefaces already belong to someone else in people's heads. Not through any fault of the drawing — through saturation of use.

A few have become welded to an entire sector. Others have become welded to an operating system or a piece of software, and using them reads as an absence of decision: the brand didn't choose a typeface, it accepted whatever was there. Others still carry a context you'd rather avoid — there are designs that can only say «children's» and designs that can only say «nineties technology».

None of this means a widely used typeface is ruined. It means that if you choose it, you're accepting the company it keeps. A classic grotesque in an architecture studio works because the company is good and the neutrality is intentional. The same grotesque on a brand that wants to feel warm and close will have to go and find that warmth somewhere else — in the photography, in the tone of voice, in the colour — and it probably won't be enough.

## The problem with pairing

Most brands use at least two typefaces — one for headings, one for running text. The commonest mistake is choosing two that are too similar, creating visual monotony, or too different, creating visual conflict. A good pairing creates productive contrast: one typeface expressive and distinctive, the other discreet and highly legible.

The most frequent version of the mistake is pairing two neutral sans-serifs. The page gains no hierarchy at all and gains a strange sense of technical error — it looks as though one of the fonts failed to load and got substituted. If the difference between two typefaces only shows up when you zoom in on the «g», it isn't a pairing. It's indecision.

The opposite version is putting together two typefaces that both want to be the lead. A high-contrast serif in headings and a strongly drawn geometric in body text compete for attention and neither wins. In a pairing, one of the two has to accept the supporting role.

Three routes that solve this in practice:

- **One family, properly explored.** Families with many weights and widths give you more variation than two badly matched families. Light for secondary data, regular for body, bold for whatever has to stop the eye, italic for nuance. It's the safest option and, done well, it isn't the poorest.
- **A superfamily.** Some families are drawn in both serif and sans versions on the same skeleton. They give you contrast of texture with no risk of disagreement, because the proportions and x-heights line up.
- **Clear contrast of function.** One expressive face for headings, one discreet and well-tested face for body. The rule is that the body face has to be almost invisible. If the reader notices it halfway through a paragraph, it's getting in the way.

When you test a pairing, don't judge it on a poster. Judge it on a page with seven hundred words, three subheadings, a list and a caption. That's where fragile pairings fall apart.

## The system before the aesthetics

Before you fall in love with a typeface, confirm that it works as a system. A beautiful typeface that fails in context is worth nothing — and the cost of finding that out late is high, because by then there's printed paper, signage on order and a website already built.

The list worth running through before you decide:

- **Weights and styles.** Does it have light, regular, medium, bold? Does it have true italics, drawn as italics, rather than an automatic slant? A brand system with two weights forces you to resolve the whole hierarchy with size alone, and that runs out fast.
- **Behaviour at small sizes.** The footnote on a contract, the ingredients table on packaging, the label on a form field. Print it and read it at eight point. Plenty of elegant typefaces close up and turn into illegible grey at that size.
- **Numerals.** Do you have tables, prices, results, timetables? You need tabular figures, which align in columns. You also need to know whether the numerals are all the same height or whether they rise and fall — the latter are lovely in running text and terrible on an invoice.
- **Characters.** Do the accents you need exist, and are they well drawn? A tilde or a cedilla in a typeface designed for English tends to be an afterthought. And if you communicate in other markets, check carons, Cyrillic or Greek characters early, as the case may be.
- **Licensing.** Does the licence cover web, app, third-party use, embedding in a PDF that will be distributed? Is your monthly traffic within the allowance? Choosing a typeface whose licence doesn't fit the real use is a legal problem disguised as an aesthetic one.
- **File weight.** Four weights and four italics on a website is eight files to load. It's worth asking whether you genuinely need all eight.

## Where the type will live

A typeface isn't chosen in the abstract. It's chosen for a set of surfaces, and some of those surfaces are hostile.

If the brand has signage, the type will be read from twenty metres away, at an angle, with low sun across it. If it has packaging, it will be printed on surfaces that spread ink and fatten thin strokes. If it has video, it will appear as subtitles over moving image, where contrast is unstable. If it has an app, it will sit alongside the system typeface in menus you don't control.

Make the list of surfaces before you open the font catalogue. Often it's that list that decides — and it decides well, because it eliminates half the candidates before there's time to grow fond of any of them.

## A test before you close

Set three things in the typeface you're considering: the brand name, the one-line sentence that describes it, and a real paragraph from your website. Nothing invented, nothing in Latin. Print it, show it for a few seconds to someone who doesn't know the project, cover the sheet and ask what kind of company that seemed to be. If the answer is «serious» and you wanted «approachable», the typeface is wrong — however much you like it.

Typography isn't decoration. It's your brand's voice translated into image, and it's the part of the visual system that appears in absolutely everything you write, from the poster to the email signature. Choose it with the same rigour you'd apply to any other significant brand decision.
