In a physical shop, a customer can pick a product up, feel its weight, read the label and examine it from every angle. Online, all they have is the photograph. Which means product photography isn't a supporting element of the e-commerce experience — it is the experience. And yet most brands treat it as a commodity, a box to tick at the lowest possible cost.

It's worth understanding what's actually happening on the other side. Someone arrives at a product page from a search or a social post, with two or three tabs open comparing alternatives. They don't know the brand. They don't know whether the material is good, whether the stitching will hold, whether the ceramic is thick or thin. They have to decide with the only information available, and that information is visual. The photograph doesn't illustrate the product: it replaces it.

## What the person is actually trying to work out

Before talking about light and backgrounds, it helps to know which questions the images have to answer. They're always roughly the same ones, and almost none of them are about aesthetics.

- **How big is this?** The question that causes the most returns. A vase photographed alone on a white background could be fifteen centimetres tall or half a metre.
- **What's the texture like?** Knit, wood grain, matte or gloss finish, the thickness of a fabric. If it can't be seen, the worst is assumed.
- **Is the colour really this?** The second biggest cause of returns. A blue that goes green in one image and grey in the next doesn't inspire confidence.
- **How is it used?** Where it fits, how it opens, what comes in the box, how much of a hand it fills.
- **Is this for me?** The least conscious question and the most decisive.

Most e-commerce catalogues answer the first question well and ignore the other four. That's where sales are lost — not in an ugly image, but in a doubt that went unresolved and got resolved with a click away from the page.

## Lighting is the difference between cheap and premium

The single variable with the biggest impact on product photography is lighting. Soft, directional light that models the shape of an object communicates quality. Flat light from above that kills texture and depth communicates the opposite. This holds regardless of the product itself — the same item shot in good light and in bad light reads as two different products at two different price points.

The reason is straightforward: light is what tells us something is three-dimensional. When the lighting comes from the front and evenly, the object loses volume and is reduced to a coloured silhouette. When it comes from the side, a gradation forms between the lit area and the shadowed area, and it's that gradation the eye reads as shape, weight and material.

### Direction, quality and shadow

There are three decisions to make, and all of them have commercial consequences.

**Direction.** Side light, or light from slightly behind, reveals relief: the weave of a fabric, the rim of a plate, the engraving on a metal clasp. Frontal light hides all of it. For products whose sale depends on texture — ceramics, leather, knitwear, wood, bread — frontal light is a strategic error, not just a technical one.

**Hardness.** A light source that's large relative to the object gives soft shadows and gradual transitions. A small source gives shadows with hard edges. Neither is wrong: leather often looks better under harder light that picks out the grain; a glass bottle or a cream jar needs diffusion so it doesn't fill up with sharp-edged reflections. The choice has to be deliberate, not whatever the room allowed that day.

**Cast shadow.** The most underrated decision of the three. A product cut out with no shadow at all floats, looks stuck onto the page and reads as generic catalogue imagery. A discreet shadow settling the object onto a surface gives it physical presence. For a lot of brands, adding a believable shadow is the most visible difference between before and after, and it requires nothing else.

### Colour has to be trustworthy

Good lighting also means accurate colour. Mixing window light with office bulbs produces images where half the product leans blue and the other half leans yellow — and the customer, who knows nothing about any of this, simply concludes that the colour can't be trusted. Shooting with one dominant light source and setting white balance against a grey reference solves the problem at the source, and saves hours of editing spent trying to rescue something that was wrong from the start.

There's a cheap test for whether your lighting is doing its job: put two images of the same type of product side by side, one of yours and one from a brand a price tier above you. If yours looks flatter, the problem is almost always light, not camera.

## Context sells the aspiration

Studio shots on pure white are necessary but not sufficient. They show the product; they don't sell the life the product makes possible. Lifestyle photography — the product in use, in an environment that resonates with the target customer — creates desire rather than just awareness. The most effective e-commerce strategies use both together.

The common mistake is treating lifestyle as decoration: one nice image at the top of the page, ornamental, adding no information at all. Context only works when it does work.

### What context solves that the studio doesn't

**Scale.** A hand holding the product, or the product sitting on a table with recognisable objects around it, answers the size question better than any dimensions table. A mug next to a book is immediate; «height: 9.5 cm» requires effort.

**Body proportion.** In clothing, there's no substitute. The same shirt on different bodies says more about the cut than ten shots on white. It's also the information that reduces returns the most.

**Use.** A product whose purpose isn't obvious needs to be seen being used. A stand, a kitchen tool, a storage piece — if the person has to imagine how it works, they often give up before they imagine it.

**Belonging.** This is the aspirational part, and it's legitimate as long as it's honest. The setting, the light, the hands, the objects around it: all of it says who the brand is speaking to. A minimal kitchen in pale stone speaks to one audience; a worn wooden table with crumbs on it speaks to another. Choosing badly here is more expensive than having mediocre images, because it actively pushes away the right people.

### A sequence that works

For most products, a well-served page has a recognisable order: one clean hero image that identifies the product unambiguously; two or three views from different angles; a close-up of material or finish; a scale image; one or two of real use. Six to eight images, each with a job. It isn't about quantity — it's about coverage. Twelve images repeating the same angle are worse than five that answer five different questions.

## Consistency is a brand signal

When every product image follows the same lighting approach, the same styling language, the same proportions and the same framing, the cumulative effect is of a brand in control and serious about quality. Inconsistent product photography — different lighting, different backgrounds, different scales — communicates the opposite, even when each individual image is technically competent.

And this point is more practical than it sounds, because an online shop is rarely seen one image at a time. It's seen as a grid. On the category page, twenty thumbnails appear together, and that's where the incoherence jumps out: one product too big inside its square, another tiny, one on pure white and its neighbour on a greyish white, two with shadows and the rest floating. Each image might be fine. The set looks like a market of different sellers.

### Where consistency gets lost

Nobody decides to have an incoherent catalogue. The catalogue becomes incoherent by accumulation: one shoot in January with one photographer, another in September with someone else, three new products shot in a rush by the founder because the campaign couldn't wait, two images sent by the supplier and used because «they're good enough».

The way to stop this is to document what was decided, and to do it while it's still fresh. A short document, one or two pages, with the essentials:

- **Aspect ratio and framing.** Image format and how much margin the product leaves inside the frame.
- **Relative scale.** Whether a large product and a small one fill the same percentage of the frame, or keep their proportion to each other. This has to be decided once; it can't be improvised at every shoot.
- **Background.** The exact value, not «white». Pure white and near-white don't sit together in the same grid.
- **Lighting setup.** Direction of the key light, whether there's fill, whether there's a shadow and what kind.
- **Styling.** What can appear alongside the product and what never does. Props, surfaces, permitted colours.
- **Post-production.** Level of cut-out, shadow treatment, limits on saturation and contrast.

With this, a new photographer can pick up someone else's work two years later. Without it, every shoot starts from zero and the catalogue becomes a harvest of styles.

### Consistent doesn't mean identical

There's a confusion worth clearing up: consistency doesn't mean every image looks the same. It means they belong to the same system. Lifestyle images can be looser, warmer, more narrative — as long as the light has the same character and the palette is recognisable. What can't happen is the rules changing for no reason, because at that point the brand stops having a visual voice and starts having accents.

## The calculation worth doing

Photography isn't a cost to minimise. For any brand selling online, it's one of the highest-leverage investments available: the same images will serve the shop, the ads, social media, the wholesale catalogue and the press, for years, every time someone encounters the brand.

And it pays back from the less obvious side too. A return isn't just a lost sale: it's shipping in both directions, the time of whoever processes it, a product that comes back in questionable resale condition and a customer who doesn't come back at all. Images that show size, colour and texture honestly prevent a share of those returns — and they also prevent the purchases that should never have happened in the first place, which are the ones that generate the worst reviews.

The question isn't whether you should invest in good photography, but how long you can afford not to.
