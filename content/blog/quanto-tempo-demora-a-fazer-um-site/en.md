It's the first question in nearly every conversation about a new website, and the honest answer is uncomfortable: it mostly depends on you.

For a brand website — an introduction, services, portfolio or products, contact, maybe a blog — a realistic timeline runs from two to four months between kick-off and launch. Online shops with large catalogues and integrations take longer. Campaign pages with three sections take far less. But the gap between two and four months isn't the studio padding its estimate: it's the difference between a project where the content arrives on time and one where it doesn't.

It's worth understanding where the time actually goes, because intuition gets this wrong almost every time. Anyone who's never built a website assumes the slow part is designing the pages. It's one of the fastest.

## Weeks 1 and 2 — discovery and structure

Before anyone opens a design tool, there are questions to answer that aren't visual ones. Who visits the site, and in what state of mind do they arrive. What has to happen for the site to be considered useful: a quote request, an application, a sale, a phone call. Which pages exist and how they connect to each other.

What comes out of this phase is a sitemap and a schematic of each page type — what's usually called a wireframe. These are deliberately ugly drawings: grey boxes, placeholder text, no colour. The ugliness is the point, because it forces the conversation to be about hierarchy rather than taste. It's far cheaper to disagree about the order of sections in a grey box than in a finished mockup.

This is also where you decide what *doesn't* go in. Almost every project starts with a longer page list than it needs, much of it inherited from an old site nobody has reviewed in years. Cutting here saves weeks later, because every page that survives will need text, images and rounds of revision.

Two weeks is normal. It can be one if the brand already has a defined strategy and there's someone available with the authority to decide.

## Weeks 3 to 5 — visual design

With the structure approved, you design how it looks. The usual approach is to start with one or two key pages — the homepage and the most important page on the site — rather than designing everything at once. The reason is practical: those pages establish the visual language, and approving it early avoids redoing ten pages when someone decides the typeface isn't right.

Once the direction is signed off, the remaining pages come quickly, because they become applications of a system that's already been decided rather than new decisions.

This phase has two scheduling traps. The first is the number of revision rounds. Two well-organised rounds work. Five rounds, each one introducing a fresh opinion from someone who wasn't in the previous ones, can double the length of the phase. It's worth deciding at the start who approves — ideally one person, at most two — and consolidating feedback into a single document per round.

The second trap is designing with fake text. A mockup full of *lorem ipsum* or invented filler always looks better than the finished page, because placeholder text is exactly the length the layout wants it to be. When the real text arrives with twice as many words, the page falls apart. Designing with real content, even provisional content, is the single decision that saves the most time afterwards.

## Weeks 6 to 9 — build

With the design approved, the site gets built. It's the longest phase in working days and, paradoxically, the most predictable: it's technical work with a defined target and no open decisions.

What happens here is more than turning images into pages. You build the reusable components that will hold the site up for years. You deal with behaviour across screens of every size, which isn't resizing but rethinking: a seven-item menu on desktop can't be the same object on a phone. You deal with the states that never appear in mockups — what shows while something loads, what happens when a form is filled in wrongly, how a section looks with one item instead of six.

Then there's the administrative part nobody remembers: the content management system, so you can edit text without depending on anyone else; the forms and where they send; the measurement tools; the redirects from old pages to new ones — forget those and you can lose search positions built over years, overnight.

Three to four weeks is typical. Integrations with external systems — invoicing, bookings, stock, CRM — can add more than the entire rest of the site, and it's the estimate that most often fails, because it depends on someone else's documentation and someone else's deadlines.

## Week 10 — testing and launch

The last week is verification. The site is tested across different browsers and devices, speed is measured, accessibility is checked, the text is proofread on every page, forms are tested end to end right through to the inbox they land in.

The launch itself is quick: point the domain, confirm the security certificate, submit the sitemap to the search engines and watch it for a few days. Small things always surface in the first week live. Reserving time for them avoids the feeling that the project is never going to end.

## The phase that delays everyone

None of this explains the eight-month projects. And there are plenty of them.

The cause is almost always the same: content. Text, photographs, service descriptions, team biographies, case studies, product specifications, client logos in a usable file format. All of it comes from the client side, and this is where timelines die.

Why? Because writing the text for your own website is one of the hardest tasks there is. It requires deciding what the company is, in how many words, and in what order of priority — which is to say it requires exactly the strategic clarity that has spent two years not happening internally. And unlike the rest of the project, there's no supplier pushing: it's a task competing with the work that pays the bills, and it loses every week.

The pattern is recognisable. The kick-off meeting goes well. The design is approved with enthusiasm. The build moves forward. And then the project enters a three-week wait for the About page, which quietly becomes seven weeks, because the partner who has to approve it is on holiday and the team photo needs reshooting now that new people have joined.

### How to treat content as part of the project

There are ways to reduce this, and all of them involve no longer treating content as something you'll sort out «when we get there».

- **Start the content before the design, not after.** The day the structure is approved, you already know which pages exist and what sections they contain. That's the moment to start writing, not weeks later.
- **Set deadlines per page, not one overall deadline.** «Send the content by the 20th» is a deadline nobody meets. «Services page by the 6th, team by the 13th» is achievable, and missing it is visible immediately.
- **Work to word counts.** A layout wants three hundred words in a section, not twelve hundred. Giving that number to whoever's writing turns a vague task into a concrete one.
- **Appoint a single owner.** Not a committee. One person who gathers, decides and delivers — even if other people write parts of it.
- **Decide early what you're doing about photography.** If there's going to be a new shoot, book it weeks ahead; it depends on weather, on people's availability and on access to locations. If you're going to use stock, commit to that in the art direction rather than discovering it at the end.
- **Consider hiring someone to write.** Delegating the copy to someone who does it professionally usually costs less than two months of delay — and a site's text is the part that most influences whether someone gets in touch or closes the tab.

And if the content isn't ready, there's a healthy decision very few people take: launch without the missing page. A live site with eight good pages is worth more than a stalled site waiting for the ninth. Pages can be added later, and the site isn't worse for having grown.

## What actually speeds things up, and what doesn't

Speeds things up: having the brand defined before you start, a single decision-maker, content begun early, feedback that's grouped and specific, and a scope agreed in writing.

Doesn't speed things up: asking the studio to work faster when the problem is somewhere else. Nor adding people to a project halfway through, which usually slows it down. Nor cutting the structure phase to «save time» — that just defers the cost into the build, where changing things is several times more expensive.

If you want a rule of thumb: a website's timeline is the content timeline plus a few weeks. Sort out the part you own and the rest is reasonably predictable.
