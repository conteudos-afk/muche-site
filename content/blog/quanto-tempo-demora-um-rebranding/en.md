The question comes up in the first meeting every time, and it deserves an honest answer: a full rebrand for a mid-sized company usually takes between three and six months, from kick-off to the delivery of the guidelines. Smaller projects — a single brand, few people deciding, limited applications — close in six to ten weeks. Projects with several markets, a portfolio of sub-brands, or board-level approval comfortably pass the one-year mark.

The number isn't the interesting part. The interesting part is understanding that design time is reasonably predictable and decision time is not. When a project runs three months over, it's rarely because somebody spent three months designing. It's because somebody spent three months waiting.

## The phases, and what each one actually consumes

### Discovery and strategy

Internal interviews, conversations with customers, competitor analysis, an audit of what already exists. At the end, a document with positioning, audience, territories and attributes — the base from which everything else is justified.

Two to four weeks on a normal project. The synthesis work is fast; the scheduling is what drags. Interviewing eight people in an organisation where nobody has half an hour free can take longer than writing the entire report. It's the first phase to slip and almost nobody accounts for it in the timeline.

### Verbal platform

Brand territory, tone of voice, sometimes a name and a tagline. One to three weeks of work — unless there's a name change, and then you're into trademark checks, domain availability and existing use in the market, all of which depend on external bodies and don't speed up because you push. If you're considering changing the name, treat it as a project with its own deadline, not as a line item inside the rebrand.

### Creative direction and concepts

This is where the visual language is born. Usually two or three distinct directions, each with its own logic, presented in enough application that you can tell what they actually are — not logos floating on a white background.

Two to three weeks to produce. Then comes the presentation, and after that the interval nobody can predict: the time until a decision exists. More on that shortly.

### Refinement

Once the direction is chosen, you tune it: construction of the symbol, versions, typography, the definitive palette, grids, photographic treatment, iconography. Two to four weeks, with two or three rounds of revision agreed at the outset.

It's the most technical phase and the most stable part of the schedule. It's also the most misunderstood from the outside, because visually very little changes — anyone watching from a distance assumes the work has been done since the concept presentation.

### System and applications

The identity applied to whatever the company actually needs: stationery, email signatures, packaging, signage, social media, presentations, vehicles, uniforms. Three to six weeks, but that range tells you very little — it depends entirely on the inventory. A consultancy with a business card and a PowerPoint deck is not a retail chain with shopfronts, menus and a fleet.

Taking an inventory of touchpoints in the first week is the single most useful thing you can do for a budget and a deadline. When the menu nobody mentioned turns up in April, that isn't an unforeseen event: it's a failure to survey properly.

### Guidelines and handover

Documentation, final files in every format, a supplier version, kits for the internal team. One to two weeks. It looks like paperwork and it's the difference between an identity that holds and one that falls apart in six months, applied by ten people with no shared criteria.

### Implementation

Production, printing, installation, website development, launch. It can be a week or it can be a year, depending on how much there is to replace. A new website almost always adds two to three months, with its own team, and it's frequently treated as though it were an extension of the guidelines. It isn't: it's a parallel project.

## Where the schedule actually slips

Add up the phases and you get a count that doesn't match what happens. It's in the gaps between them that the time disappears.

### The void after the presentation

The concept presentation goes well. Everyone reacts, discusses, points to preferences. And then nothing happens, because the commercial director wasn't there and has to see it before any decision can be made. A new session is booked for a fortnight later. She prefers a different direction. Now there are two opinions, and the person who decides would rather not upset either side.

This void is the biggest consumer of time in identity projects, and it always comes from the same place: nobody established at the outset who decides. If the answer is «we all decide together», the project will take more than twice as long as planned and the brand will come out of it weaker, because consensus removes precisely the angles that made it distinctive.

Before you start, there are three questions to ask. Who approves. Who is consulted but doesn't approve. How long until there's an answer after each presentation. If the third answer is vague, add a month to the schedule and you'll have a more realistic estimate.

### Feedback collected in corridors

The most expensive version of a comment is the one that arrives in loose pieces over two weeks: an email on Monday, a line on WhatsApp on Wednesday, a remark from the partner over lunch on Friday. Each piece generates a small change. No piece knows about the others. After ten of them, the work is going in circles and nobody can reconstruct how it got there.

The alternative is simple and requires discipline: reactions are consolidated on the client side, in a single voice, on a single date. Even if there's internal disagreement — especially if there is. The internal argument has to happen inside the company, not inside the creative process.

### The committee that appears halfway through

Project approved by the founder, running well, and in week seven somebody decides to show it to the team. Fourteen people see, for the first time, work built on three months of context they don't have. Their reactions are legitimate and unanchored: they draw on each person's private taste, not on the approved strategy.

This isn't an argument against involving the team. It's an argument for doing it early and with framing. Involving people in discovery, as a source of information, works well and improves the outcome. Involving them in concept evaluation, with no context, turns a strategic decision into a vote on taste.

### The strategy that was never really approved

There are projects where the strategy phase was approved out of politeness. The document was dense, nobody openly disagreed, everyone moved on. The disagreement is stored away and comes back later, disguised as a visual comment: «I don't know, this feels too serious to me». That isn't a comment about typography. It's somebody who never accepted the positioning.

Reopening strategy during refinement costs weeks and forces you to throw away valid work. Which is why it's worth forcing the discomfort at the right moment: when the platform is approved, ask explicitly what each person disagrees with, and keep pushing until somebody says something. A yes that comes too easily at this stage is almost always a deferred no.

### Third parties who aren't on your calendar

Printers, signage manufacturers, vinyl suppliers, embroiderers, trademark registries. None of them works at the pace of your project and most of them enter late. A colour proof can take two weeks. A shopfront depends on council permits. A trademark registration has its own timelines and they aren't negotiable.

The way to protect the schedule is to contact them during the design phase, not after. Knowing what your printer can actually do influences identity decisions — and it's better to know before the decision is made.

### The launch date that was already promised

An industry trade fair, a company anniversary, a product launch. The date has existed from the beginning and sometimes it was never mentioned to the people doing the design. When it surfaces, everything is compressed: what needed four weeks now has two, the guidelines get pushed to later, and the applications are done in a rush.

A fixed date isn't a problem, as long as it's declared in the first meeting. With it in view, you plan backwards and define what goes into the launch and what goes into phase two. Without it, the project is designed against a deadline that was never real.

## How to shorten it without ruining it

There are legitimate ways to save time, and nearly all of them sit on the organisation's side, not the design side.

- **Appoint a single decision-maker.** They can consult whoever they like. They decide alone. It's the single measure with the biggest impact on the timeline.
- **Book every meeting at the start.** With names, dates and compulsory attendance. Scheduling as the project advances adds invisible weeks.
- **Do the touchpoint inventory in week one.** Everything that carries the brand, including the things nobody remembers.
- **Agree how many revision rounds exist.** Two or three, per phase. With no limit, the process doesn't end — it just stops out of exhaustion.
- **Separate the website from the rebrand.** Two projects, two schedules, a clear sequence.
- **Decide what isn't included in this phase.** Not everything has to change at once. The fleet can wait for natural renewal.

There are also ways of shortening that only look like shortening: skipping strategy, asking for five concepts instead of two, seeing one more direction «just to compare», doing without the guidelines. All of them trade weeks now for months later, and the last one trades them for an identity that comes apart on its own.

## The deadline is a consequence

When somebody asks how long a rebrand takes, they're usually asking how long the creative part takes. That part can be estimated with reasonable precision and it's rarely the problem.

The rest of the timeline is a consequence of organisational decisions: who decides, how quickly, and on what basis. A company that can answer those questions rebrands in three months. One that can't takes a year — and comes out with a worse brand, because every week of indecision files down one more edge.
