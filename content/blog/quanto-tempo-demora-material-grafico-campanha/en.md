People asking how long a campaign's graphic materials take are usually thinking about the poster. The key visual, the one that goes on the wall and that everyone will see. It's a single piece, it looks quick, and sometimes it genuinely is.

What takes time is everything else. It's the same visual on a billboard, on a half-page magazine ad, across three banners at different dimensions, vertical for stories, square for the feed, as an event cover, as an email signature, as a roll-up for the trade fair, front and back of a flyer, as vinyl for the shop window, and in two languages. The idea was approved in an afternoon. Rolling it out takes weeks.

This article is about that gap — and about how to estimate a timeline without ignoring it.

## The phases that always exist

Whatever the size of the campaign, graphic work goes through the same five phases. What changes is how long each one lasts, not whether it happens.

**Brief and framing.** Working out what the campaign is saying, to whom, for how long and in which media. This phase is short in days and decisive in consequences: a vague brief doesn't shorten anything, it just pushes the decisions to the end, when they're expensive.

**Concept.** The visual idea. Typically one to three proposals, applied to two or three representative formats so you can see how the thing behaves in different places. This is the phase with the most thinking and the fewest files.

**Developing the key visual.** Once the route is chosen, it gets refined. Final photography or illustration, the right typeface, hierarchy resolved, versions with and without the claim, how the logo behaves, what happens when the legal text runs long. This is where you build the rules every other piece will follow.

**Rollout.** Applying the visual to every format on the list. It's the longest phase in almost every campaign and the most underestimated in almost every timeline.

**Print-ready artwork.** Preparing files for whoever produces them: colour profiles, bleed, resolution, outlined fonts, file names the printer will understand, each supplier's specifications. Technical work, barely visible, impossible to skip.

## Why the number of formats weighs more than the difficulty

There's a wrong instinct that delays a lot of campaigns: the assumption that time sits where the creative difficulty sits. In practice, time sits where the repetition sits.

Designing a difficult visual is a problem you solve once. Adapting it to twenty-two formats is a problem you solve twenty-two times — and each of those times brings its own small decisions, which nobody can fully automate.

### Why adaptation isn't copy and paste

Anyone who has never done adaptations assumes you resize the artwork and you're done. It doesn't work like that, for three reasons.

- **The proportions aren't compatible.** A visual conceived as a vertical poster doesn't fit a long horizontal banner. The image has to be recomposed, the text reorganised, the hierarchy rethought. It isn't the same design at a different scale: it's another design carrying the same idea.
- **Reading distance changes everything.** A billboard is read from ten metres away, in motion. A half-page magazine ad is read from forty centimetres, standing still. The same information, across those two formats, demands different type sizes, different levels of detail and different amounts of message. Replicating without adjusting gives you an unreadable billboard or a print ad set in circus type.
- **Every format has its own technical demands.** Safe areas, text limits, file formats, maximum weights, space for mandatory information. A digital ad that exceeds the permitted file weight gets rejected by the platform, and you find that out on delivery day if nobody checked beforehand.

### The cost of starting and stopping

Even if every adaptation were trivial, there'd be a fixed cost per piece that nothing eliminates: opening the file, setting up the document, exporting, naming, checking, archiving, listing. Multiply that cost by forty pieces and you have days of work that produced no new ideas at all.

This is why two campaigns with the same creative ambition can have completely different timelines. One runs to eight pieces. The other runs to sixty, because there's a trade fair, because there's a vehicle fleet, because there are shops, because there's a partner, because there's an English version.

## What usually blows up the calendar

A small set of variables explains most delays. They're worth discussing before you fix dates, because every one of them is knowable on day one.

- **Formats that appear halfway through.** The initial list had twelve pieces. Nine more arrived because somebody remembered the October event and because sales promised materials to the distributor. It's the single biggest cause of overrun, and it's always presented as «just a quick thing».
- **Image production.** If the campaign depends on new photography or original illustration, there's an entire chain before design even starts: casting, location, permissions, shoot, selection, retouching. With stock, days. With your own production, weeks — and design sits waiting.
- **Approval by committee.** When three departments give their views in sequence, design time is a fraction of the calendar. Four people at two days each is eight days per round.
- **Legal text and compliance.** Mandatory mentions, terms and conditions, funding logos, small print that arrives after the layout is closed. Each of these things wrecks tight compositions and forces you to redo pieces that were already approved.
- **Languages.** Each additional language isn't a translation, it's a new composition. German runs longer, English runs shorter, and there's always a line that no longer fits where it used to.
- **External production lead times.** Printing, screen printing, banner installation and large-format production have their own calendars, and they don't negotiate. The artwork date isn't the campaign date: it's the campaign date minus factory time.

## How to estimate without guessing

A useful estimate doesn't start with a number of days. It starts with a list.

### Write the list of pieces before you ask for a timeline

Every piece, with dimensions, with languages, with variations. Not «social media» — how many posts, in which formats, static or animated. Not «in-store materials» — which windows, which dimensions, how many shops have different measurements.

That list is the most important document in the campaign and it's usually the one that doesn't exist. Making it takes a morning and it's the only way to get a timeline that means anything.

### Split the list into three piles

**Pieces that require decisions.** The key visual and the two or three formats where the idea has to be tested. Low volume, a lot of time per unit.

**Pieces that require recomposition.** Formats whose proportions force you to rethink the arrangement. Medium volume, medium time.

**Pieces that require execution.** Size variations within the same logic, language versions, direct rollouts. High volume, little time per unit — but a lot of units.

A realistic calendar puts the first pile at the start, makes the second depend on the first being approved, and reserves a continuous block for the third that can't be interrupted by changes to the concept. Touching the key visual after the third pile is done means redoing all of it.

### Count backwards from the date it goes up

The date that matters isn't the layout approval. It's the campaign being out in the world. Between one and the other sits printing, delivery, installation, platform validation, media scheduling. Work backwards: go-live day, minus production, minus artwork, minus rollout, minus approvals, minus concept. What's left is the time actually available. Often the exercise reveals that the calendar was already compromised before anyone opened a file.

## Orders of magnitude

No invented numbers, but with some honesty about proportions, there are recognisable patterns.

A small campaign — key visual, a handful of digital formats, one printed piece — lives in weeks rather than months, provided the imagery exists and there's a single decision-maker.

A medium campaign, with print, digital, in-store and an event, tends to need several weeks on the rollout phase alone, even if the concept was approved first time.

A large campaign, with dozens of formats, its own photography production, more than one language and approvals spread across departments, is measured in months — and the creative part occupies the smallest slice of that time.

The rule of thumb: the concept phase tends to be the shortest of the three visible phases, the rollout the longest, and the print-ready artwork the easiest to forget in the calendar.

## What genuinely shortens timelines

Not everything that looks like acceleration accelerates. Asking for more rounds in less time shortens nothing; it just makes the result worse. What actually shortens things is more boring.

- **Close the list of formats on day one.** Every piece added after the concept is approved costs more than it would have cost at the start.
- **Have a single decision-maker.** That doesn't mean ignoring everyone else. It means one person gathers all the comments and returns a single answer, instead of four contradictory opinions on four different days.
- **Have the final text before the final layout.** Setting placeholder copy is normal during concept and extremely expensive during rollout.
- **Resolve the imagery early.** Photography, illustration, licences. Until the imagery is settled, no piece can be finished.
- **Build the visual with adaptation in mind.** A concept that depends on one very specific composition adapts badly. A concept with a clear system — where the logo goes, how the headline behaves, what's fixed and what's flexible — rolls out fast. That decision is taken at the start and you either pay for it or save on it at the end.
- **Book the approval diary up front.** Review dates in everybody's calendar before work starts. It's mundane and it rescues more timelines than anything else.

## The mistake that keeps repeating

The usual conversation about timelines focuses on what's difficult and ignores what's plentiful. Weeks get spent debating which of the three proposals is best, and then forty adaptations are expected in three days because «the idea is already approved».

The idea being approved is the start of the work, not the end. What comes next is less interesting to watch, doesn't appear in portfolios, and is exactly what determines whether the campaign reaches the street intact or reaches it with three rushed pieces nobody had time to check.

When you ask for a timeline, bring the list. That's the difference between an estimate and a guess.
