Events generate photographs. Almost every company that runs a conference, a product launch or a client dinner walks away with hundreds of images. Very few walk away with a story. The difference lies entirely in the brief and in the intent the photographer brings to the day.

This isn't an equipment problem or a talent problem. There are very capable photographers producing galleries nobody ever opens again, because nobody told them what the images were for. Without that information, the photographer does the only sensible thing available: covers everything. Covering everything is a defensive strategy — it guarantees nothing is missing, and it also guarantees nothing stands out. The result is an archive, not a narrative.

## Decide the narrative before the event

Before the day, ask: what story is this event telling? What do we want someone who wasn't there to understand and feel when they see the images? The answers shape everything — which moments the photographer prioritises, which details they catch, which people they follow.

The question sounds abstract and isn't. It has very concrete answers, and they differ enormously from each other. If the story is «this is the conference where the industry meets», the images that matter show a full room, corridors mid-conversation, people greeting each other — and a lone speaker on a well-lit stage adds almost nothing. If the story is «this brand brings together a small, selected group of clients», the images that matter are close: tables, hands, half a dozen people leaning over the same thing — and a wide shot of the room proving there were fifteen people in a space built for a hundred works against you.

There's a useful test before you close the brief: can you describe three images that, if they don't exist by the end of the day, mean the event was badly covered? If the answer is generic — «the atmosphere», «the people» — there's no narrative yet. If it's specific — «the moment the founder greets the longest-standing client», «the room seen from the back with the screen lit», «the hands signing» — the photographer already knows where they need to be and at what time.

## The hierarchy of moments

Not all moments at an event are equal. There are the official moments — the speaker on stage, the award being handed over, the ribbon being cut — and then there are the real ones: the conversation in the corridor, the genuine reaction, the small detail that captures the atmosphere. A great event photographer knows the second category is often worth far more than the first.

The official moments have to exist. They're the record; they serve the report, the press, the sponsor who needs to see the logo. But they're also the most predictable and the most interchangeable between events: a speaker on a stage is a speaker on a stage, whatever the brand. They rarely move anyone and they never distinguish anything.

The real moments are what make that event that event. Some examples of what that means in practice:

- **The before.** The empty room with the chairs aligned, the badges still waiting on the registration desk, the team testing the microphone. These are images of preparation and anticipation, and they give the gallery a beginning.
- **Reaction rather than action.** While the speaker talks, half the story is in the room: someone laughing, someone taking notes, two people exchanging a look when the thing everyone was waiting for finally gets said. Photographing the people listening usually yields more than photographing the person talking.
- **Hands.** Hands picking up a product, pointing at a screen, pouring, signing, applauding. These are shots that tell a story without needing faces — which also makes them useful when there are restrictions on showing people.
- **The breaks.** Coffee is where people behave like people. There's more truth in fifteen minutes of a break than in ninety minutes of a panel.
- **The detail of the space.** The typography on the wall, the signage, the branded glass, the light coming through the window at six in the evening. This is what gives the gallery visual coherence and ties the images back to the brand's identity.

None of this is an argument against the official shots. It's an argument for deciding, in advance, how much of the shoot goes to each category. If nobody decides, the stage eats the entire session, because the stage is the thing with a scheduled time and the thing that's already lit.

## The brief is a collaboration

Share the event programme in advance. Walk the space with the photographer. Identify the people and the moments that absolutely have to be captured. Discuss the intended use of the images. The more context the photographer has, the better the decisions they make in the moment — and at an event almost every decision is made in the moment, because there's no second chance.

**The programme, with the real timings.** Not the version that went out with the invitation: the internal one, with the time the doors open, the time the board member arrives, the time the group photograph has to happen before someone leaves. It's worth marking on the programme the three or four points where the photographer cannot be anywhere else.

**A recce of the space.** Walking the room beforehand — ideally at the same time of day — solves problems that otherwise get discovered too late. Where the backlight from a large window falls. Which backgrounds are usable and which are full of cables, fire extinguishers and crates. Whether the stage has enough light or whether you'll be working at high sensitivity. Where a quick portrait corner can be set up without blocking circulation. Whether the ceiling is low and dark, which limits the use of bounced flash.

**The must-have people.** A list with names and, ideally, photographs. A photographer who has never seen the managing director doesn't know she's just walked in, and nobody notices until the following day, when the image doesn't exist. It also helps to have someone from the organisation available to point discreetly: «that one over there is the client we talked about».

**The intended use of the images.** Social media needs a different framing from a printed report. If the photographs are going to live in vertical format, they need to be shot vertically, not cropped from horizontals in a hurry at the end. If they're going to be covers with text over them, the framing needs clean areas left deliberately empty. If they're going to large-format print, the exposure approach changes. If they're going to feed a website for a whole year, more neutral shots matter — without the date and without the sponsor's roll-up banner in every corner.

**The restrictions.** This is the part of the brief that feels most awkward and saves the most time. Who can't appear in images. Whether there are minors present and what the procedure is. Whether there are areas, screens or documents that can't be photographed. Whether there's a competing brand, or a partner who has since left, that can't appear in the background. You find this out before, or you find it out afterwards, with the selection done and half the good images unusable.

## The edit is half the job

A one-day event easily produces many hundreds of files. Delivering all of them isn't generosity: it's passing the problem along. Nobody inside the company is going to scroll through a thousand thumbnails looking for the right image, so the gallery stays exactly where it is.

A delivery that's actually useful is edited with a narrative criterion. It has an opening, it has a sequence that moves through the day, it has variety of scale — wide shots, mid shots, details — and it doesn't repeat the same situation fifteen times with minimal variations. It usually works well in two stages: a small set, processed and delivered quickly, for whoever needs to publish while the event is still news; and the full selection, worked on calmly, for the archive and for the uses that come later.

It's also worth thinking about how what you hand over is organised. Folders by moment, readable file names, a small selection flagged as «the best» for anyone who only wants ten. It's unglamorous work and it's what determines whether the images get used again in March.

## The images live beyond the day

An event happens once. The photographs can feed communication for months: an announcement for the next edition, a recruitment page showing what it's like to work there, the sales deck, the founder's profile, the cover frame for a podcast episode recorded that day. That future use is only possible if it was anticipated — and anticipating it costs one half-hour conversation before the event.

Event photography, done well, extends the life and the reach of the event itself. Images that tell a story get shared, remembered and reused. Images that merely document get filed and forgotten.
