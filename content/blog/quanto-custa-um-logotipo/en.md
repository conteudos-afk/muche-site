You ask five different places to quote for a logo and you get numbers that don't belong in the same conversation. Thirty euros on an online marketplace. Three hundred from a freelancer. Two and a half thousand from a small studio. Twelve thousand from an agency. None of them is lying. They're simply selling different things under the same name.

This is where most people get lost, and reasonably so. In almost everything we buy, a higher price means more of the same thing — more hours, better materials, a longer guarantee. With a logo, what changes between the tiers isn't the amount of drawing. It's the amount of thinking that happens before the drawing and the amount of system that exists after it.

## What you're actually buying

A logo, on its own, is a file. A competent person can draw one in three hours, and sometimes it comes out well. What costs money is everything around it.

Before the drawing: understanding what the company does, who it sells to, at what price, against whom, and what it wants people to feel. This isn't presentation rhetoric. It's what allows someone to choose between two visual directions without falling back on «I prefer this one». Without that foundation, the decision gets made on taste — and the taste of the person paying is not, as a rule, the taste of the person buying.

After the drawing: the versions that make the logo usable. Horizontal and vertical. Symbol on its own. A version for small sizes with the fine detail stripped out. Behaviour on dark backgrounds and over photography. Vector files a vinyl supplier can actually open. Colours defined for screen, for print, for embroidery. Written rules.

A logo without that second part isn't finished. It's only drawn. The difference shows up on the first day you need it at 15 mm on the spine of a package, or engraved in one colour on a van door.

## The tiers, and what fits inside each one

The figures below are orders of magnitude in the Portuguese market, not a price list. They shift with the city, the experience, the sector and the complexity of the job.

### Tens of euros: generators and marketplaces

Automated platforms and international micro-work marketplaces. You get a file quickly, sometimes the same day.

What you're buying is a shape pulled from a library, combined with a common typeface and your company name. In many cases the same symbol has already been sold to other people, in other countries and other sectors. There's no real exclusivity, rarely a clear transfer of rights, and usually no editable vector file.

What's it good for? Validating an idea before there's a business. A side project that might die in six months. Putting a name on a document while you decide whether the thing is going ahead. In those situations it's a perfectly rational decision, as long as you know it's temporary.

It's no good for registering a trade mark, for printing in volume, or for building any kind of recognition. A symbol that isn't yours can't become yours.

### Hundreds of euros: a freelancer early in their career

Someone makes an original drawing for you. There's a conversation, two or three proposals, some revisions.

What usually exists at this level: originality, availability, enthusiasm. What usually doesn't: the strategic part and the systemic part. The brief is short, the choice between proposals comes down to preference, and the delivery is a set of files with no usage rules. Often there's no reduced version, no guidance for dark backgrounds, and no conversation about what happens when the brand grows.

It's an honest tier for a small business with few touchpoints — a local service provider, a brand that lives mainly on social media, a project with a single product. The risk isn't the quality of the drawing, which can be good. It's the drawing being good without being right, because nobody defined what right meant.

### Low thousands: experienced designer or small studio

Here the process starts to have phases. There's a proper opening conversation, a look at what competitors are doing, proposals presented in application — not logos floating on white, but the logo on a shopfront, on a package, on a phone screen. There are refinement rounds agreed up front and an organised handover: versions, a palette defined with values by substrate, typography chosen and licensed, a short usage document.

This is the tier where most small and medium Portuguese companies solve the problem well. You're not buying a fifty-page strategy report, but you are buying judgement. Someone who can tell you why one direction serves your positioning better than the other, and who can defend the choice without resorting to taste.

### Thousands to tens of thousands: identity with strategy

At this level the logo is one deliverable among many, and it isn't even the most expensive to produce.

In come internal interviews, conversations with customers, an audit of what already exists, definition of positioning and verbal territory. In comes a complete visual system — typography, colour, grids, iconography, photographic direction, composition principles. In come the company's real applications, which might be fifteen or a hundred and fifty. In comes a usage manual written to be consulted by suppliers and internal teams. Sometimes a new name, with registry and domain checks.

The price rises with the inventory, not with the prestige. A retail chain with shopfronts, menus, a fleet and uniforms carries an implementation cost that a consultancy with business cards and slide decks never will. And it rises with the number of people deciding: a project with a board and three markets consumes more coordination time than drawing time.

## What legitimately pushes the price up

If you get two very different quotes, it's worth working out which of these variables explains the gap.

- **Groundwork.** Whether or not there was an understanding phase before anyone opened a drawing programme.
- **Number of applications.** Stationery is cheap. Signage, packaging, vehicles and uniforms are not.
- **System complexity.** A single brand is one thing. A brand with sub-brands, product lines or business units is another, and it needs architecture rules.
- **Exclusivity and rights.** Full transfer of usage rights has value and isn't always included. Read what the contract says.
- **Typography.** A family licensed for web, app and print costs money. A custom-drawn typeface costs a great deal more.
- **Preparation for registration.** Checking for prior marks and preparing files for submission to the INPI or the EUIPO is extra work, and it's the kind of work that prevents serious problems.
- **Number of decision-makers and rounds.** Every layer of approval adds weeks, and the weeks are in the price.

## What you shouldn't pay for

Some things sell well and are worth little.

**The number of proposals.** «I'll give you ten concepts» isn't generosity: it's an absence of judgement. Ten proposals mean nobody committed to a direction and the choice was pushed onto you. Two or three distinct directions, each with a logic you can explain in a sentence, are more useful than ten variations.

**Unlimited revision rounds.** They look like protection and they're the opposite. A process with no limit doesn't end — it stops out of exhaustion, usually on a version worse than the third one.

**The geometric construction pages.** The ones where the symbol appears overlaid with circles and golden proportions, proving it was built on a harmonic grid. They're pleasant to look at and they have never been used to do any actual work.

## Where cheap gets expensive

Not always. There are small brands with cheap logos perfectly suited to what they need. But the breaking points are predictable.

The file isn't vector and the signage supplier redraws it by hand, badly. The symbol has fine detail and disappears when reduced to 18 px in the favicon. There's no dark-background version and someone improvises one on presentation day. The typeface wasn't licensed for commercial use. The symbol is too close to another already registered in the same class and the application is refused — after it's already in the window.

And then there's the cost nobody counts: doing it again. A brand that changes identity after eighteen months throws away the recognition it had started to build, reprints everything and pays twice. If there's a serious chance the business will grow, the right calculation isn't what the logo costs — it's what the logo costs plus the probability of redoing it.

## How to decide what to spend

Three questions help more than any price table.

**How much does it cost to change later?** If your brand lives in digital files, changing is cheap. If it lives in packaging moulds, building façades and a vehicle fleet, changing is ruinous. The more expensive it is to change, the more it's worth getting right first time.

**How many surfaces does the brand have?** Make a list of everything that carries the logo, including the things nobody thinks to mention — the stamp, the invoice, the menu, the name badge. The length of that list is the best indicator of the budget you need.

**Is the brand a selling argument or an identifier?** There are businesses where the customer chooses on price, proximity or recommendation, and the brand only has to be recognisable. There are others where perceived quality is the sale. In the first case, spending a lot is waste. In the second, spending little contradicts the price you're charging.

## A direct answer

If you need a number to start thinking with: an original logo, with usable versions and minimum usage rules, made by someone with experience, usually sits in the low thousands of euros in Portugal. Below that, you're buying the drawing without the system. Above that, you're buying the system and the strategy that justifies it.

None of the three options is wrong. What's wrong is thinking they're the same thing at different prices.
