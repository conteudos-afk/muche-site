Colour is one of the most immediate and most powerful tools a brand has. Before anyone reads your tagline or works out what you do, they've already registered a feeling from the colours in front of them. This happens in milliseconds, and it shapes everything that follows.

It's also the identity decision most often made for the worst possible reasons. The logo gets argued over for weeks; the colour gets settled in a single sentence — «I think it works in blue» — and then stands for a decade.

## Emotion arrives before logic

Colour arrives before form and long before words. It's the first signal the eye processes and the last one it forgets. You recognise a brand from the far end of a supermarket aisle by the block of colour, not by the name, which at that distance is unreadable. The same thing happens in a grid of search results, in a feed, on a shelf.

Warm tones like gold and amber suggest heat, quality and tradition. Cool blues communicate trust and precision. Deep greens evoke nature and ethics. None of this is accidental in the brands you admire — it's engineered.

But there's a second layer colour carries that gets talked about far less: colour states the price. A saturated orange, a bright yellow and a loud red read as volume, promotion, urgency — and they work extremely well for businesses that sell that way. The same products in desaturated tones, with little contrast between them and plenty of space around them, read as expensive. This isn't a matter of good taste. It's a convention learned over decades, and your customer reads it without knowing they're reading it. If you want premium positioning and you've chosen a discount palette, you're contradicting your own price before you've even shown it.

## The myth of the meaning chart

There's a simplified version of this subject that has been circulating for years: blue is trust, green is health, red is passion, black is luxury. It has the advantage of being easy to memorise and the flaw of being almost always wrong when applied without context.

Colour has no fixed meaning. It has meaning in relation to three things.

- **The category.** Green on a yoghurt brand reads as natural and lightly processed. The same green on a bank reads as money, or as nothing at all. Blue in a clinic reads as hygiene and competence; on a wine label, it reads as odd.
- **The competition.** What a colour means is partly what separates it from the colours sitting next to it. A single red on a shelf of white packaging doesn't communicate what a red surrounded by five other reds communicates.
- **The execution.** The same colour on uncoated paper, on glossy plastic, or projected on a screen is not the same experience. A gold printed in ink is beige; the same gold in metallic foil is something else entirely.

The practical conclusion is simple: don't choose a colour from a list of adjectives. Choose it from the position you want to occupy in a specific category, alongside the colours already there.

## Look at the field before you choose

Before deciding a brand's colour, it's worth doing an exercise that is tedious and extremely useful: lay out the identities of the main competitors side by side, as blocks of colour only, with no logos and no names. In almost every category a pattern appears. Technology and finance live in blue. Healthcare oscillates between blue and green. Organic food lives in green and kraft brown. Luxury property lives in black, beige and gold.

From there, only two decisions are available, and both are legitimate.

**Belong.** Use the category colour because you need to be immediately recognised as part of it. A new clinic, an insurer, any service where strangeness costs you trust. Here the differentiation happens somewhere else — in the typography, the photography, the tone — and colour does the work of credentialing you.

**Stand out.** Take the colour nobody in the category uses, accepting the risk of looking out of place. This works when the brand has something genuinely different to justify the misalignment. It works badly when the colour is the only different thing, because then the brand just looks eccentric.

The mistake isn't choosing one or the other. The mistake is choosing without having looked.

## Owning a colour

Some of the most valuable brands in the world have, in practical terms, claimed a colour as their own territory. This isn't about registering a shade as a trademark — it's about being so consistent and so present in one colour that the association becomes automatic. That level of recognition is enormously valuable, and it takes years of disciplined application to build.

Three conditions tend to be present when it happens.

**The colour is simple to hold in mind.** Heavily nuanced tones, the result of complex mixes, are hard to remember and nearly impossible to reproduce from memory. A colour you can name in one word has an advantage over one that needs three.

**The colour appears as surface, not as detail.** A brand doesn't come to own a colour by having a coloured logo. It owns it when the colour fills the whole box, the whole shopfront, the whole page, the whole uniform. A brand colour that lives only inside the symbol is invisible from three metres away.

**Nothing changes for years.** The seasonal rebrand is the direct enemy of this. Every shift in tone resets the counter. Brands that own a colour are, almost without exception, brands that resisted the temptation to update it.

It's worth saying what this implies: it's a long-term goal, not a launch outcome. A two-year-old brand is not going to own a colour. What it can do is start behaving as though it already does.

## The mistake most brands make

The most common colour mistake is choosing shades based on personal preference rather than strategic intent. Your favourite colour may not be the colour that best serves your positioning.

The symptom is easy to spot: the conversation about colour uses the verb «like». I like this green. I don't like orange. My wife thinks purple is heavy. None of these sentences contains any information about the customer you're trying to attract, about the category, or about the price you charge. When they turn up, change the question from «what colour do you like?» to «what colour makes the right customer feel what you want them to feel?». The answer is usually a different one, and occasionally uncomfortable — there are founders who end up with a brand in a colour they can't stand, and it's a good brand.

The second most common mistake is inconsistency: using slightly different shades in digital and in print, or letting teams apply the colour without clear guidelines. This one is more mundane and does more damage, because it accumulates.

The places where colour slips are always the same. The RGB value on the website doesn't match the CMYK on the business card. The Pantone reference was chosen on a screen and nobody ever saw the printed swatch. The window vinyl is a shade no supplier can repeat. The embroidery on the uniforms uses whatever thread range the manufacturer had in stock. The background of the PowerPoint deck is an approximate blue somebody picked with an eyedropper from a compressed image.

In isolation, each deviation is imperceptible. Together, they produce a brand that looks almost the same everywhere — and «almost the same» is precisely what a company that doesn't control its own output feels like.

## The part of the palette that's always missing

An identity needs more than a primary and a secondary colour. It needs a system that survives real life, and the part left out is nearly always the same:

- **Neutrals.** The greys, the beiges, the off-whites. This is where most of the surface of a website or a package actually lives. A slightly warm neutral and a slightly cool neutral produce completely different brands, and this decision is rarely taken — it's inherited by default.
- **Functional colours.** Success green, error red, warning yellow. If you don't define them, a developer will use the defaults from whichever library is to hand, and the brand ends up with colours nobody chose.
- **Interface shades.** Hover states, borders, dividers, form field backgrounds, disabled states. Dozens of values that end up improvised if the palette has only three entries.
- **Dark background versions.** Plenty of brand colours work on white and vanish on black. You need to decide what happens, rather than finding out on the day somebody builds a dark presentation.

And then there's contrast. A brand colour often works for buttons and headings and works badly for body text: yellows and oranges on white are practically illegible, and light greys on white are an aesthetic choice and a poor one. The rule is practical — the palette has to include a pair of colours with enough contrast to read, and that pair may not be the pretty pair.

## Decided once, protected always

A reasonable route to getting there has three steps. First, define the emotional territory you want to occupy, in words, before looking at a single colour. Second, map what the competition does and decide consciously whether you want to belong or to stand out. Third, test the colour in real application — a package, a page, a simulated shopfront — and not on a moodboard, because a shade that's beautiful in a five-centimetre square can be unbearable when it covers a wall.

Then write all of it down: Pantone, CMYK, RGB and hex values, substrate by substrate, with the proportions of use and the cases where each colour should not appear. And decide who approves a deviation, because sooner or later a supplier will tell you they can't reproduce that exact shade.

Colour should be a decision made once, with rigour, and protected from then on.
