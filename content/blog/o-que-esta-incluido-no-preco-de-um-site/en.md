You get two quotes for the same website. One is three times the price of the other. The natural reaction is to assume somebody is overcharging, but almost always something else is going on: the two documents aren't proposing the same work. One includes content, the other expects you to deliver it. One includes training, the other leaves you sitting in front of a system nobody explained. One includes three rounds of revisions, the other includes one and bills the rest by the hour.

The difference between a complete quote and a quote with holes in it isn't the final figure. It's the number of decisions left untaken — because every deferred decision is a cost that turns up later, usually at the worst possible moment, which is the week before launch.

This article is a map of what makes up the price of a website, and where the gaps usually are.

## Design

It's the part everyone pictures when they think about a website, and the part where proposals diverge most without saying so.

The first question is how many screens are actually designed. A ten-page site doesn't need ten designs — but it needs more than two. There's the homepage, the service page, the long-form content page, the listing, the article, the form. If a proposal talks about «homepage and inner page design», it's worth asking how many different inner templates that covers. When the answer is one, it means all the content on the site will have to fit into the same shape, and some of it won't.

The second is what happens on a phone. A website isn't a drawing that shrinks. On a small screen the hierarchy changes, the menu changes, tables break, three-column grids become one. If mobile design isn't in the quote, somebody will improvise it during development — and improvised is exactly how it will look.

The third is states. A button has a resting state, a hover state, a pressed state, a disabled state. A form has empty fields, filled fields, fields with errors, a success message. A listing might return no results. None of this shows up in a pretty mockup and all of it exists on the real site. When it isn't designed, it gets decided by default in the code, and the brand you worked on so carefully ends up speaking in the voice of the framework.

Finally, revisions. A serious proposal says how many rounds it includes and what a round is. Without that, any project can drag on indefinitely, and both sides end up resentful: one for never being satisfied, the other for never being finished.

## Development

This is the easiest part to underestimate, because it's the least visible to the person buying.

Building the pages is the obvious work. Around it sits a set of things that are either included or billed separately:

- **Content management.** Being able to edit the site yourself isn't automatic. A site can be delivered fully editable, partly editable, or completely closed. This is probably the most common price difference between quotes that look identical.
- **Forms.** Where the information goes, who receives it, what happens next, how GDPR is handled, how spam is blocked. A form that sends an email is simple. A form that feeds a CRM, segments contacts and fires an automatic reply is a different job.
- **Integrations.** Newsletter, bookings, payments, maps, social feeds, analytics. Each one takes time and each one can break when the provider changes its API.
- **Multilingual.** It isn't duplicating pages. It's URL structure, a language switcher, content that expands to different lengths depending on the language, and a workflow for keeping versions in sync. If there's any chance of an English version a year from now, it's cheaper to plan for it than to patch it in later.
- **Performance and accessibility.** A site can be delivered working and still be slow or unusable with a keyboard. If it isn't written down that there's work on these fronts, assume there isn't.
- **Testing.** In which browsers, on which devices, at which screen sizes. «It works» is an elastic phrase.

Then there's a structural question that rarely appears in proposals and that determines the cost of the next few years: what is it built in, and who can maintain it. A site built on a common platform can be picked up by plenty of people. A site built on an agency's own closed system can only be picked up by that agency. Neither option is wrong, but the second carries a price that isn't in the initial quote.

## Content

This is where most projects slip, and almost never because of the people writing code.

A website needs text, images, video, icons, and an information structure that decides what appears where. Plenty of quotes assume, in one discreet line, that «content is supplied by the client». That line looks harmless and it's the source of half the delays.

Writing the text for a website isn't drafting a few paragraphs. It's deciding what the company says first, how it describes itself to someone who has never heard of it, which services get prominence and which sit in the background. It's positioning work disguised as writing work, and that's why it always gets pushed to next week — because it's difficult, not because nobody has time.

Images have the same problem wearing different clothes. A site designed for strong photography and fed with photos taken in a rush isn't the site that was approved. It's a worse version of the same layout. If the brand doesn't have its own image library, either budget for a shoot or design the site knowing that — and designing for very few images is perfectly possible, as long as it's a decision and not a surprise.

It's also worth asking who migrates the old content. Moving two hundred articles from one site to another, with images, internal links and redirects, is real hours of work. When nobody owns that task, it ends up being done by whoever has the least availability in the company, at night, with mistakes.

## Training and handover

A website delivered without explanation is a website that will age fast.

Handover should include, at minimum, a session with whoever is going to touch the site: how to create a page, how to publish an article, how to swap an image without breaking the layout, what should never be changed. Ideally recorded, because the person who receives the training isn't necessarily the person who'll still be in the company in two years.

It should also include short documentation — not a fifty-page manual nobody reads, but instructions for the five or six tasks you'll genuinely carry out.

And it should include access. All of it. Domain, hosting, admin panel, third-party service accounts, code repository. In your company's name, not in the name of whoever built the site. This isn't distrust: it's hygiene. A relationship with an agency can end perfectly well and you can still have a problem if the domain is registered on somebody else's credit card.

## What comes after launch

The price of building a website isn't the price of having a website. Recurring costs always exist, and it's worth seeing them before you sign.

- **Domain and hosting.** Annual amounts, usually small, but somebody has to pay and renew them.
- **Licences.** Web fonts, premium plugins, form or search tools. Some are priced per site, others by traffic volume.
- **Maintenance.** Security updates, backups, checking that nothing has broken. Not optional on a site with an admin area.
- **Support.** How long after launch fixes are free, and what counts as a fix. A bug that already existed at handover is a fix. A new page is new work. That boundary should be written down.
- **Evolution.** A useful website changes. It's worth knowing from the start how small changes get commissioned — hourly, on a retainer, in packages.

## Questions that make two quotes comparable

If you have different proposals on the table, half a dozen questions resolve almost all of the ambiguity:

- How many distinct page templates are designed, and does that include mobile versions?
- How many rounds of revision are included, and what counts as a round?
- Which parts of the site will I be able to edit myself?
- Who writes the copy and who handles the images?
- Who migrates the existing content and sets up the redirects?
- What's included in training and documentation?
- What recurring costs exist from day one?
- For how long are fixes free, and what counts as a fix?

With those answers, the cheapest quote is sometimes still the cheapest. Other times it stops being cheapest — and it becomes obvious why.

## The price that matters

A complete quote isn't the one with the most line items. It's the one that leaves few decisions untaken, because every decision left open will get made in a hurry, by someone who wasn't expecting it, with money or time nobody had planned for.

When you compare proposals, compare what each one is missing. It's almost always more informative than comparing the numbers at the bottom.
