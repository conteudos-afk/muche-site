There's no price list for visual identity, and anyone publishing one is selling something else. But there's a concrete reason the same question gets such different answers: «visual identity» doesn't mean the same body of work in every proposal. In one, it's a logo and two files. In another, it's a complete system, documented, ready to be used by people who have never spoken to you.

Comparing prices without comparing scope is the source of almost all the confusion. Before you ask what it costs, it's worth understanding what you're comparing.

## Why the range is so wide

Three things explain most of the variation between quotes.

**The number of applications.** A consultant with a business card, an email signature and a presentation template is not a restaurant group with menus, takeaway packaging, uniforms, shopfronts and interior signage. Drawing the symbol takes the same time in both cases. Everything else doesn't.

**The number of people deciding.** A founder who decides alone consumes a fraction of the time taken by a board with five opinions and a commercial director who only shows up at the third session. That time is work, and it either appears in the price or it appears later, as overrun.

**The depth of the strategy phase.** Some projects start with a two-page brief and a positioning that's already settled. Others need internal interviews, conversations with customers, competitor analysis and a written document that will justify every decision that follows. These are different projects with different prices, and both can be called visual identity.

On top of that, the obvious: solo studio, small agency, large agency, international network. Each tier has its own structure, costs and pace, and none of them is automatically the right choice.

## What goes into a complete budget

The visible part is the symbol. The part that takes up most of the hours is everything else.

### Strategy and platform

Positioning, audience, territory, attributes, sometimes naming and tone of voice. It's the phase that produces nothing to show, and the one that determines whether there's any criterion for judging what comes next. Without it, concept presentations turn into votes on personal taste, because there's no shared yardstick.

When a quote is very low, this is almost always the first thing that isn't in it.

### Logo and system design

The symbol, the wordmark, the lockups. And then the part that rarely appears in the presentation:

- Horizontal, vertical, reduced and symbol-only versions, with rules for when each one is used.
- Minimum size, in millimetres and in pixels.
- Clear space, defined using a unit taken from the logo itself.
- Behaviour on light backgrounds, dark backgrounds, brand colour and photography.
- The single-colour version, for embroidery, stamps, engraving and every supplier who doesn't work with gradients.

A logo delivered without this is a drawing. A system is the drawing plus the rules that let it survive being applied by other people.

### Colour

Far more than two primary colours. Hex, RGB, CMYK and Pantone values, because these aren't automatic conversions of each other — a Pantone chosen on screen and never seen in print is a mistake that costs a reprint. Defined neutrals, functional colours for success, error and warning, interface tones, dark-background versions and pairs with enough contrast for body copy.

If the palette has three entries, someone will invent the other thirty while the website is being built.

### Typography

Choice of families, full hierarchy with sizes, weights, line heights and letter spacing. Fallback fonts for when the licensed one isn't available — in email, in shared documents, in decks made by people who don't have the family installed.

And then there's the licence, which is a separate cost and which many quotes leave out without saying so. A commercial typeface has a price per seat, a price per web traffic tier, sometimes a price per app. It's worth knowing who holds the licence, what it covers and what renewal costs before the brand becomes dependent on it.

### Applications

This is where the budget becomes specific. The difference between a five-thousand quote and a twenty-thousand quote is almost always in this line, not in the quality of the symbol.

What usually goes in: stationery, HTML email signature, presentation template, proposal or invoice template, social covers and formats. What adds volume: packaging, labels, signage, shopfronts, vehicles, uniforms, point-of-sale material, stands, merchandise.

Making an inventory of this in the first week is the most useful thing you can do for a budget. When the menu nobody mentioned turns up halfway through the project, it isn't an unforeseen event: it's a gap in the brief, and it will be invoiced.

### Photography and imagery

Photographic direction — what light, what framing, what treatment, what never appears — is one thing. Producing a shoot is another, with its own costs: photographer, crew, studio or location, models, usage rights. These are separate items and should appear separately.

### Manual and handover

Documentation, final files in every format, a versioned and organised folder, a simplified kit for the internal team. It looks like admin. It's the difference between an identity that still holds together after two years and one that falls apart because ten people opened the wrong file.

### Rollout and supervision

Talking to printers, approving colour proofs, checking vinyl, overseeing signage installation. It may be included, it may be billed hourly, it may not exist at all. Whichever it is, it has to be written down, because it's real work and somebody will have to do it.

## What a very low price is usually hiding

A quote substantially below the others doesn't necessarily mean poor quality. It almost always means less scope. It's worth finding out where.

**There's no strategy.** Work starts straight at the drawing stage, from a short brief. That can go well if the positioning is already clear in your head and you can explain it. It goes badly if you were expecting the project to clarify it for you.

**There's one direction, not three.** A single route is presented. That's not a flaw in itself — some studios work that way by conviction, with a strong alignment phase beforehand. But it's different from choosing between distinct territories, and it changes what happens if you don't like it.

**Revisions are limited or absent.** One round included, the rest extra. Legitimate, if it's stated. Expensive, if you find out halfway.

**There's no system, just a logo.** You get the symbol files and nothing else: no documented palette, no type hierarchy, no rules. Every decision that follows will be taken by whoever builds the site, whoever makes the leaflet, whoever does the shopfront. Each with their own judgement.

**There's no manual.** The identity lives in the head of the person who designed it. While that person is available, it works. After that, it doesn't.

**The font isn't licensed in your name.** This gets discovered late, usually when the site goes into production and an unbudgeted invoice appears.

**The file is a single flat image.** You get a PNG or a JPEG and nothing more. With no vector, no SVG, no versions, no serious supplier can work with it, and the first shopfront will be redrawn by whoever cuts it.

**Rights aren't clarified.** Who owns the identity once it's paid for? Can it be used with no limit on time, territory or medium? For identity work, full transfer of rights is the reasonable standard. If the contract doesn't say so, ask.

The question is never whether the price is low. It's whether what's inside the price is what you need. There are companies for whom a well-drawn logo, two files and a palette are perfectly adequate for the next two years. For them, paying for a complete system is waste. The problem is buying the minimum package while believing it's the complete one, and discovering the difference when the first supplier arrives.

## What else costs money, and doesn't appear in the quote

There are real costs that aren't fees, and they're worth adding up before you decide.

- **Typeface licences**, as above, with renewals.
- **Trademark registration**, with fees per class and possible legal support. If you're changing name, start this on its own timeline.
- **Production.** Printing stationery, manufacturing signage, shopfront vinyl, uniforms, packaging. Usually the largest slice of the total investment, and none of it is in the design budget.
- **Photography and video** to feed the identity once it's launched.
- **The website.** Almost always a parallel project with its own schedule and budget. Treating it as an extension of the manual is the most common way budgets blow up.
- **Your own internal time.** Meetings, gathering information, consolidating feedback. It isn't invoiced, but it exists.

## How to compare quotes without fooling yourself

Put them side by side and look for the same five answers in all of them.

- **What's delivered, item by item.** Not «complete visual identity», but the list. If one proposal has a list and another has adjectives, you already know something.
- **How many creative directions and how many rounds of revision.**
- **How many applications, named one by one.**
- **What happens to anything left out.** Price per hour, per piece, per retainer.
- **Rights, licences and file formats.**

If two proposals answer all of this the same way and the prices are very different, then the conversation really is about the value of the studio. In most cases they don't answer the same way, and the price difference is explained long before you get to that argument.

## Where it makes sense to spend, and where it doesn't

If the budget is limited — and it usually is — it's better to do fewer things properly than many things superficially.

Spend on clarity of positioning, even in a short version. Spend on a solid system of logo, colour and typography. Spend on a manual, even a short one, as long as it's specific. Spend on the three or four applications your company produces every week.

Postpone the applications you aren't going to produce yet. Postpone the vehicle fleet, which can change at natural renewal. Postpone the merchandise. Postpone the website, if it means a whole project that doesn't fit right now.

And resist the temptation to ask for five concepts instead of two to «get more out of the investment». More options don't produce better decisions — they produce committees, indecision and a result that tries to please everybody. The identity that comes out of a process like that is almost always the least distinctive of the five.

The price of a visual identity doesn't measure the quality of the drawing. It measures the amount of work, the depth of the strategy and the number of pieces that end up ready to use. Once you understand exactly what you're buying, the question stops being whether it's expensive and becomes whether it's what you need.
