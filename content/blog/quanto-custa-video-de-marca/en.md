It's the first question almost everyone asks, and the one that always gets the worst answer. «It depends» is true, but it's useless. What matters is understanding what it depends on, because then you can look at two very different quotes and know what one has that the other doesn't.

A video isn't a product with a list price. It's a set of hours from skilled people, spread across three phases, plus equipment and on-the-ground costs. When one quote is half another, it isn't because somebody is being greedy. It's because one of the two has fewer days, fewer people, or less time in the edit — and it's worth knowing which.

## The budget is, nearly always, people's time

There's a persistent idea that the cost of a video sits in the equipment. It does, but that's the small part. A camera rents for a day; a director of photography who knows what to do with it doesn't rent at that price, and they're the one who decides whether the material arriving at the edit is usable at all.

Most of the money in a brand video is human time: hours of writing, hours of preparation, hours on the shoot day, hours in the edit. Everything else — cameras, lights, lenses, vehicles, drives — is a fraction. This explains something that confuses a lot of clients: why a three-minute video can cost the same as a thirty-second one, or less. Final duration isn't the variable. The number of days and people is.

## What actually moves the price

There are only a handful of variables and they're always the same ones. When you ask for quotes, these are what you should compare.

### The size of the crew

One person, alone, with a camera and a microphone, can shoot a decent interview in a room. What they can't do is set up lighting, operate the camera, watch the sound, direct the person speaking and handle logistics all at once. Something drops — and it's usually the sound, because it's the thing least noticed in the moment and the thing that ruins everything afterwards.

Crews grow out of necessity, not vanity. A day with several locations needs somebody handling production, or the day slips. A scene with camera movement needs an assistant. Multiple contributors need somebody on sound full-time. If there are people on camera who don't work for the company, you get hair and makeup, wardrobe, releases. Each of these roles adds a daily rate to the budget, and each of them exists because somebody, on an earlier production, found out what happens without it.

### The number of shoot days

This is the easiest variable to compare and the one that weighs most. A shoot day costs you the entire crew, the equipment hire, the travel, the food, and the time to set up and pack down. Two days cost very nearly double.

And the number of days isn't a question of ambition: it's a consequence of what you asked for. Three locations in different cities don't fit into one day. A factory that can only stop production on a Saturday morning forces a day dedicated to that alone. A brand that wants to show all four seasons forces four trips across a year. When one quote is cheaper than another, the first place to look is the number of days allowed for — and then ask what doesn't fit in them.

### Post-production

This is the phase most underestimated by whoever is paying, and the one that varies most between proposals. Editing isn't cutting: it's watching all the material, choosing, building a structure that holds up, adjusting the rhythm, and then going back because the structure wasn't right.

Inside post-production there are layers that stack up:

- **The edit.** The base work. It depends on the volume of footage and how structured that footage is. A day of interviews shot without a script generates far more edit hours than two days shot against a shot list.
- **Colour correction and grading.** Correction makes everything consistent — it makes shots filmed at different times of day look like they belong to the same piece. The grade gives it character. The first is mandatory; the second is where a video starts to look like something that was thought about.
- **Sound.** Noise cleanup, balancing voices, atmospheres, the final mix. It's the work nobody sees and everybody feels. A video with average images and good sound passes; the reverse doesn't.
- **Music.** A library track with a commercial licence costs one thing; original composition costs something else entirely. The difference isn't only aesthetic, it's legal — and the wrong licence is a problem that only surfaces once the video is already circulating.
- **Graphics and animation.** Simple name captions are quick. An animated logo, infographics or moving typography are a different discipline, with a different hourly rate.
- **Subtitles and versions.** A square version, a vertical one, a fifteen-second cut, burnt-in subtitles, another language. Every version is additional time, and it's almost always forgotten in the brief.

### The revision rounds

This one rarely appears in proposals and always should. A serious proposal states how many rounds of changes are included. It isn't there to restrict the client: it's so both sides know that the tenth round has a real cost.

Revision rounds are also where a budget runs away without anybody deciding to let it. If the video passes through five people who were never part of the original conversation, each with an opinion, the edit goes round in circles. Deciding who approves — one person, with the final word — saves more money than any negotiation over price.

### The things that aren't creative and cost anyway

There's a layer of costs that produces not a single shot and without which there's no shoot at all: travel and accommodation, insurance, filming permits for public or private space, image releases, archive drives, catering. On a production with exteriors and travel, this layer can be significant. In a proposal where it simply doesn't appear, either the whole job genuinely happens in one room, or somebody forgot it and it will turn up later.

## Why the lowest quote usually ends up expensive

Nobody picks the cheapest option out of carelessness. You pick it because, on paper, the two quotes appear to promise the same thing. The difference is that one of them cut something — and the cut is almost never flagged.

### Preparation was cut

This is the most common cut, because it's the most invisible. No written script, no location recce, no shot list, so you turn up on the day and improvise. The result isn't a visible disaster: it's a collection of reasonable shots that tell no story. The edit inherits raw material with no direction and returns a video that works technically and says nothing.

And this is where cheap becomes expensive, because the only way to fix it is to shoot again. Which is to say, pay twice.

### People were cut from the day

A crew that's too small doesn't fail through incompetence. It fails through physics: there aren't enough people to do everything in the hours available. Sound gets left to the camera's onboard microphone. The lighting stays as it was. You shoot fast so everything fits. Then, in the edit, you discover the best answer in the interview has a lorry driving over the top of it, and there's nothing to be done.

### The edit was cut

A rushed edit always shows, even to people who can't explain why. The rhythm is uneven, cuts land in the wrong places, the colour doesn't match between shots, the sound jumps in volume from one sentence to the next. The video doesn't look bad: it looks amateur. And that's exactly the reading that gets applied to the brand it represents.

### The video doesn't do what you needed

The worst outcome isn't a badly made video. It's a well-made video that doesn't do the job. You end up with a handsome two-and-a-half-minute film when what you actually needed was three short pieces for social, a vertical version and a fifteen-second cut for a campaign. That isn't a quality problem — it's a brief problem, and it gets solved beforehand, in conversation, not afterwards.

A video that sits in a drawer cost you a hundred per cent of what you paid.

## How to read two proposals side by side

When you have two quotes with very different numbers, compare them like this:

- **How many shoot days?** If one has one day and the other has two, you already know half the difference.
- **How many people on the day, and in what roles?** Ask for the list. «Production crew» isn't an answer.
- **How much post-production time is allowed for?** In days, not in adjectives.
- **What's included in the deliverables?** Formats, durations, versions, subtitles, archive files.
- **How many rounds of changes?** And what the next one costs.
- **What do the music and image licences cover, and for how long?**
- **Who owns the raw footage at the end?**

If both proposals answer all of that identically and the numbers are still far apart, then yes, there's a conversation to have about price. In most cases they don't answer identically — and the difference stops being a mystery.

## The right budget is the one that matches the right job

There's no correct figure for a brand video. There's a correct figure for the video you need, and that only emerges once you've defined what it has to do, where it's going to live and how long it has to last.

The most reliable route to controlling cost isn't asking for a discount. It's reducing scope deliberately: fewer locations, fewer contributors, fewer versions, one idea instead of three. A smaller video done well is always worth more than a big one done at half strength — and it takes less explaining when the time comes to show it to somebody.
