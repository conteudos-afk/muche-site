A website can be technically excellent — fast, accessible, logically structured — and still leave visitors completely indifferent. Function is the floor, not the ceiling. The websites that convert and retain visitors do something beyond working correctly: they create a feeling that matches what the brand is promising. That feeling is designed. It doesn't happen by accident.

It's worth being clear about what we mean by emotion, because the word has been used to justify a lot of decoration. We're not talking about friendly illustrations or entrance animations on every block. We're talking about something simpler and harder: someone who opens the website of a handmade jewellery brand and the website of a financial software company should feel two different things before reading a single line of text. If they feel the same thing, the design has failed — even if everything works, everything loads quickly and everything passes the audits.

## Function is the floor, not the ceiling

The technical work is the entry requirement. A site that's slow or confusing never even gets the chance to provoke anything: the person leaves first. But once that's solved, it stops being a competitive difference. Almost every serious brand's website now loads in acceptable time, works on a phone and has predictable navigation. The difference between the site that gets ignored and the site that gets remembered is decided somewhere else.

That somewhere else is made of small decisions, nearly all of them invisible in isolation: how long a transition lasts, how many pixels separate a heading from the paragraph below it, whether the button says «Submit» or «Let's talk», whether the black is black or a very dark grey. No visitor notices any one of these decisions. All of them together produce what they later describe as «I liked it» or «I don't know, it didn't do much for me».

## Motion as communication

Subtle animation — how elements arrive on screen, how interactions respond to the cursor, how transitions between states feel — is one of the most powerful tools available for communicating a brand's personality. A brand built on precision and quality should move differently from a brand built on energy and spontaneity. Motion design is brand design.

In practice, this comes down to three variables we decide at the start of a project and then respect across the whole site.

- **Duration.** Short transitions, around two tenths of a second, read as efficiency and control. Longer transitions, closer to half a second, read as calm, weight, deliberation. The common mistake isn't choosing badly: it's choosing a different duration in every component, because they were built in different weeks. At that point the site isn't communicating anything — it's hesitating.
- **Easing curve.** Movement that starts fast and decelerates at the end feels mechanical and safe. Movement with a slight overshoot at the end, pulling back a little before it settles, feels human and playful. The same animation, with the same duration, changes character entirely just from this. A consultancy can't have buttons that bounce. An ice cream brand can't have menus that open like a vault door.
- **Distance.** How far an element travels when it appears. Small displacements, a handful of pixels, are almost subliminal and give a sense of solidity. Large ones draw attention to the effect itself — and a site full of blocks rising twenty centimetres up from the bottom of the screen ends up talking about itself instead of talking about the brand.

There are two principles we apply without exception. The first: motion should respond, not announce. The most effective animations are the ones that happen as a result of something the person did — a hover state, a form field taking focus, a button confirming it received the click. Those make the site feel alive and attentive. Animations that happen simply because the page loaded tend to delay reading and irritate anyone arriving for the second time.

The second: motion is a preference, not a given. There are people for whom large animations cause genuine physical discomfort, and their operating system lets them say so. Respecting that preference, by reducing or removing the movement, is part of the job — and it isn't a concession, because a site that works without animation is a site where the animation is adding something rather than holding it up.

## Spacing communicates confidence

Generous white space is the most immediately legible signal of a premium brand online. It says: we're not afraid to leave room. We trust that what we have is enough. Tight, information-dense layouts communicate anxiety. Room to breathe communicates confidence.

The problem is that space is the first thing to get sacrificed. Nobody decides to build a cramped site; the site becomes cramped because four more messages arrived on the homepage in the third round of revisions, and each one stole margin from the last. Which is why spacing is a strategic decision before it's a visual one: you can only give room to what's there if the client accepts that not everything fits.

Two things help keep this standing up over the course of a project.

**A scale, not loose values.** We define a small set of spacing measurements and use only those. When every section has its own improvised margin, the eye doesn't understand why, but it senses something is out of step. When there's a scale, the vertical rhythm of the page becomes predictable — and predictability is one of the few things the brain reliably reads as quality.

**Proximity with meaning.** Space isn't only there to air things out: it's there to group them. A heading sitting close to its paragraph and well clear of the next block explains the structure of the page without a single dividing line. When every gap is identical, the page manages to be airy and illegible at once — a sequence of elements with no hierarchy, where nothing belongs to anything.

## Typography at scale

Headings on the web aren't just large text — they're the first and often the only thing a visitor reads before deciding whether to continue. The scale, weight and line height of a heading set the emotional register of the entire page. Getting this right is as much a brand decision as a typographic one.

The most frequent mistake is treating a heading like enlarged body copy. It isn't. The line height that works in a sixteen-pixel paragraph is excessive in a sixty-pixel heading: the heading falls apart into loose lines and loses its sense of being a single block. The letter spacing that's neutral at small sizes looks slack at large ones. A big heading almost always wants tighter line height and a slight reduction in letter spacing — and it's that adjustment, which nobody sees, that separates looking composed from looking dumped on the page.

Then there's register. A heading in capitals, medium weight, letters spaced apart, says one thing: institution, rigour, authority. The same words in lower case, heavy weight, very large, with the lines nearly touching, say something else: confidence, informality, presence. Neither is better. Only one of them is the brand you're working on.

And there's line length. A paragraph that stretches across the full width of a laptop screen is uncomfortable to read, and the discomfort doesn't get blamed on the layout — it gets blamed on the text, which starts to feel heavy going. Limiting the width of text columns is one of the cheapest and most effective interventions available on any website. It has a direct emotional consequence: text that's easy to read feels like a brand that cares about you.

## Contrast decides where you look

Contrast isn't only light against dark. It's large against small, full against empty, many against few. It's the tool that determines the order in which a page gets read — and therefore what a person learns about the brand in the first few seconds.

The most common symptom of missing contrast is the page where everything is important: three buttons with identical visual weight, four headings at the same size, five blocks with equal prominence. A page like that isn't neutral; it's indecisive. And indecision reads as a lack of confidence, in exactly the same way as someone who can't tell you what their company's main service is.

There's also a kind of contrast that isn't optional: legibility contrast. Light grey text on white is a recurring aesthetic choice and a bad one. It excludes people who don't see well, it excludes anyone reading in sunlight, and it carries a sense of something unfinished — as though the text were still a draft. Elegance never justifies making words hard to read.

## Tone is part of the design

A site can have impeccable typography and generous spacing, and undo all of it with a button that says «Submit». Interface copy is part of designing the emotion, and it's where a lot of projects lose the coherence they earned everywhere else.

The places where this gets decided are small and almost always forgotten in the brief:

- **Buttons.** «Send message» doesn't say the same thing as «Let's talk». One is an operation, the other is an invitation.
- **Error states.** The moment someone fills a form in wrongly is the moment the brand shows itself. «Error: invalid field» blames the person. «The country code is missing» helps them.
- **Empty states and confirmations.** The page that appears after a form is submitted gets read with total attention. It's the last thing the person takes away.
- **The 404 page.** Nobody plans to visit it, and everybody visits it once.

This isn't creative copy written to show off personality. It's the same brand speaking consistently at every moment, including the dull ones.

## Coherence is what you feel

Each of these decisions, on its own, is small. What produces the sense that «this feels right» is the decisions agreeing with each other: the motion having the same character as the typography, the spacing carrying the same calm as the tone of voice, the contrast supporting the same hierarchy the page structure proposes.

The test we run before considering a site finished is simple, and it isn't technical. We show a page to someone who doesn't know the project, for a few seconds, cover the screen and ask two questions: what does this company do, and what kind of company does it seem to be. The first answer assesses the structure. The second assesses everything this article is about — and it's usually the one that brings bad news.

The websites we're proudest of are the ones where clients tell us they can't quite explain why, but it simply feels right. That feeling is the product of hundreds of small decisions taken with care and consistency. It's invisible when it works. It's immediately obvious when it doesn't.
