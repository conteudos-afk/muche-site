Asking what a catalogue costs is like asking what a building job costs: it depends entirely on what goes inside, and anyone who answers with a number straight away is guessing. But there is a structure behind the quote, and once you understand it you can make choices rather than simply accept or refuse.

A catalogue has three separate accounts that usually arrive bundled into a single line: design, content and production. They're three different kinds of supply — even when one company handles all of it — and each follows its own logic. It's worth knowing which of the three is driving your budget before you start cutting.

## Page count matters less than you think

It's the first question everybody asks and the one that gives the least useful answer. A 32-page catalogue doesn't cost twice a sixteen-page one, and sometimes it doesn't cost much more at all.

The reason is that the heavy work in an editorial project happens at the front. Defining the grid, choosing the typeface and its weights, deciding how a product entry behaves, what happens when a description runs long, how sections are separated, how the double-page spread works, what sits in the footer. That gets solved once. After that, applying those decisions to page twenty costs a fraction of what it took to reach page three.

What pushes the price up isn't the number of pages: it's the **number of different page templates**. A 48-page catalogue in which forty pages follow three repeating templates is a calm project. A sixteen-page brochure where every spread is designed from scratch, with unique compositions, is an expensive one — and that's frequently what happens with corporate brochures, where every section wants its own special treatment.

So when you ask for a quote, say how many pages you have *and* how many distinct types of content exist. They're different pieces of information, and the second one explains the price far better than the first.

There's also a technical quirk worth knowing. Printing works in signatures, usually of eight or sixteen pages. A 34-page catalogue forces a new signature to be opened for two pages. It almost always makes more sense to fill the remaining fourteen than to pay for a half-empty signature. Asking your printer what page count is most efficient before you lock the pagination is one of the most profitable questions available to you.

## Photography is nearly always the biggest line

If your catalogue has products in it, photography is probably the largest slice of the budget — and it's the one that most surprises anyone budgeting for the first time, because it doesn't occur to anybody when they think about «making a catalogue».

A product shoot carries considerable fixed costs: setting up the studio, getting the lighting right, making the first reference images. After that, each additional product costs relatively little. That's why photographing eighty references in one go is incomparably cheaper per image than photographing ten now and ten in two months' time. If there's one decision that saves real money on a catalogue, it's concentrating the photography.

But not every product needs the same treatment. Sort them before you book the shoot:

- **Anchor products.** The ones that sell, the ones that open a section, the ones that get a full page. These deserve time, atmosphere, angle variations, possibly a use context.
- **Catalogue products.** The majority. They need one clean, accurate image, consistent with the others. They get shot in series, with the same lighting setup, quickly.
- **Secondary products.** Colour variants, accessories, references almost nobody looks for. Often a single view or a cut-out on white does the job.

Paying for anchor-product treatment across two hundred references is the most expensive mistake you can make on a catalogue.

If your catalogue isn't about products — a services brochure, a corporate document — photography changes character but doesn't go away. Here the decision is between commissioned photography and stock. Stock is cheaper and reads as stock: generic people in offices that aren't yours. Half a day, well directed, in your own premises with your own people will carry an entire brochure and has a disproportionate effect on credibility. If the budget only stretches to one thing, let it stretch to that.

## The text doesn't write itself

This is the line item that most often vanishes from the budget and most often delays the project. Somebody has to write the descriptions, the section headings, the introduction page, the captions. And «somebody» is usually an internal person who already has a full-time job.

There are two ways this goes wrong. In the first, the text arrives late, the designer lays out with *lorem ipsum*, designs everything, and then discovers the real descriptions are three times longer — which means rebuilding whole pages. In the second, the text arrives exactly as it came out of the spreadsheet, with irregular lengths and no editorial rule, and the layout looks ragged because the content was never written for the mould.

The fix is cheap and almost nobody applies it: **set a maximum length for each field before anyone writes**. Product name up to this many characters, short description up to this many, long description up to this many. Then write inside those limits. The page looks better and the designer stops playing Tetris.

## Printing only takes charge when the run is large

There's a clear dividing line here, and it determines whether it's worth having a conversation about paper at all.

On short runs — a few hundred copies — digital printing handles it, the cost per copy is high but the total is modest, and printing is usually the smallest of the three accounts. In that scenario, saving on paper saves you almost nothing and ruins the whole piece.

On long runs, the logic inverts. Offset has a significant setup cost that then dilutes, and beyond a certain volume paper becomes the dominant factor. When you're printing thousands of copies, every gram of paper multiplies — in the material price and in the shipping cost, which plenty of people forget about until the courier invoice arrives.

The variables that move the printing price, in order of impact:

- **Run length.** It determines the technology and, with it, the whole cost structure.
- **Paper weight and type.** A cover heavier than the inside pages is standard and you notice it. Special, textured or uncoated papers can cost several times the ordinary stock.
- **Format.** A non-standard size wastes sheet. Asking the printer which formats make best use of the sheet can cut a meaningful slice without changing anything about the design.
- **Finishes.** Spot varnish, embossing, foiling, die cutting. Each adds a pass through the machine. They're the easiest element to cut and the one that reads strongest when applied well in a single place.
- **Binding.** Saddle stitch is the economical option and has a page limit. Perfect binding costs more and gives the piece the presence of an object.

## Where there's genuine room to save

Not all cuts cost the same in quality. These are the ones worth making:

**Concentrate the photography into one shoot.** Already said, but it's the biggest. Plan the whole catalogue before you photograph the first product.

**Reduce the number of unique layouts, not the number of pages.** Pages add paper; layouts add hours. If you need to cut, remove exceptions, not content.

**Choose the format that uses the sheet well.** Invisible saving, zero creative cost.

**Spend the special finish in one place only.** A spot varnish on the cover says more than effects scattered through the inside pages, and costs a fraction.

**Design the document for dual use.** If the same file is going to serve print and a digital PDF, decide that at the outset. Adapting a printed catalogue for screen afterwards is new work; preparing both versions in parallel is a small addition.

And the cuts that end up expensive: dropping the paper weight on a piece meant to be handed over in person, trimming the revision rounds to the point where nobody properly checks proofs, and mixing supplier images in alongside your own photography — because the difference in lighting between the two is visible from across a room and turns a careful catalogue into a collage.

## The cost nobody budgets for

Proofreading. A catalogue with hundreds of references carries prices, codes, dimensions and availability, and all that data comes from somewhere. If it arrives wrong, it arrives wrong at the printer — and a pricing error discovered after printing isn't fixed with an update: it's fixed with a reprint or a sticker.

Someone has to check the laid-out document against the original source, field by field. It isn't the designer's job; they see what they drew, not what's missing. It's the job of whoever knows the products, and it takes longer than it looks. Put time for it in the schedule and sign off only once it's done.

## How to ask for the quote

Ask for the three blocks separately: design, content (photography and text) and production. With distinct lines you can compare proposals and see where your money is going. With one line, you can do neither.

And decide first what the piece is for. A catalogue that lives at a trade fair, passes from hand to hand and has to survive a suitcase is not the same object as one posted to twenty important clients. The first wants durability and quantity. The second wants presence and can spend its entire budget on fifty copies. Those are strategic decisions, and they're what sets the price — not the spreadsheet.
