People who ask for a podcast quote usually ask for the price of an episode. It's the wrong question, but an understandable one: that's how you budget a video, a photo shoot, a website. The problem is that a podcast isn't a piece of work — it's a commitment to a calendar. And the cost of a calendar isn't calculated by multiplying the price of one episode by the number of episodes.

It's worth understanding where the money actually goes, in order of visibility. What you see is the studio and the editing. What you don't see is the production around each conversation and the energy required for the fifteenth episode to come out as good as the second. That last item is where almost every show dies — not for lack of budget, but for lack of foresight.

## The studio: buy, rent or improvise

The first budget decision is where you record, and there are three routes with very different cost structures.

**Renting a studio.** You pay by the hour or the half-day, with or without an engineer. The advantage is that the acoustics are already solved, the equipment is good and somebody knows what they're doing. The disadvantage is that every recording carries a fixed marginal cost, which pushes you to book few sessions and squeeze them. In practice that's a good thing: it forces you to batch-record, which is the single best production habit there is.

**Building your own space.** Decent microphones, an interface, headphones, boom arms and, above all, acoustic treatment for the room. The investment is concentrated entirely at the start, but the marginal cost of each recording drops to roughly nothing. It makes sense if you're going to record for years and if you need flexibility in scheduling. It makes no sense for an experimental season of eight episodes.

**Improvising.** It's the most commonly chosen route and the one that most often produces a show nobody finishes. That said, improvising isn't fatal if you're honest about what you're doing. Two reasonable microphones in a room with curtains, a rug and full bookshelves, with mouths a hand's width from the capsules, will give you a perfectly publishable result. What doesn't work is a glass-walled meeting room with the microphone half a metre away.

The question to ask isn't «how much does the best equipment cost», but «what is the quality floor this show can't drop below». Fix that floor, reach it by the cheapest means you can find, and put the rest of the money into editing and consistency.

### The hidden cost of remote recording

If some of your guests aren't in the same room, there's an extra budget line: a local recording platform, where each participant records their own track on their own computer and the tracks upload to the cloud afterwards. It's a modest monthly subscription and it isn't optional. Recording the audio from a video call is saving twenty pounds to ruin a conversation that can't be repeated.

Add to that the time spent teaching the guest to use the tool, checking their sound before you start, and dealing with the case where one of the tracks failed. It isn't money, but it is somebody's time — and somebody's time is the largest line item in any podcast.

## Editing is where the show gets made and where the budget slips

A ninety-minute conversation doesn't give you a ninety-minute episode. It gives you, with luck, forty-five good ones. Getting to those forty-five means listening to all of it, marking what's worth keeping, cutting the rest, balancing levels between unequal voices, cleaning up noise nobody noticed in the room, assembling the theme and transitions, and doing a final quality pass.

Editing time varies enormously depending on the state of the recording. A well-led conversation with clean sound and separate tracks edits quickly. A rambling conversation recorded in a bad room can take several times the duration of the raw material — and most of that time goes on rescuing something that should have been avoided in the first place. This is why spending properly on the recording usually works out cheaper than spending little: what you save at the microphone you pay back in edit hours, month after month.

There's a clean budget decision to be made here: light editing or serious editing.

- **Light editing.** Trim the top and tail, balance the levels, add the theme. It's fast and cheap. It suits conversational formats where the rambling is part of the charm and where the audience is already won.
- **Serious editing.** Reordering, cutting within answers, removing verbal tics with judgement, shaping the pace. It costs several times more and it's what separates a show from a recording of a meeting. For a brand podcast that's starting out and has to earn attention, this is where the money works hardest.

### Transcripts, clips and everything else

Almost every show eventually wants transcripts — for show notes, for search, for repurposing text. Automatic transcription is cheap and good enough for internal use; if it's going to be published, it needs a human pass, and that's another hour per episode.

Vertical clips for social are the line item most often promised and least often delivered. Each decent clip means choosing the moment, framing the video (if there is video), captioning and checking. If you want three per episode, budget three per episode — or don't promise them.

## Publishing: cheap in money, expensive in attention

Distribution itself costs very little. A podcast host with an RSS feed is a low monthly subscription, and it's the host that delivers the show to Spotify, Apple Podcasts and everywhere else. That's the end of the cheap part.

What costs is everything you wrap around the file. An episode title that makes somebody tap — not the guest's name followed by their job title. A summary that says what the listener gets out of those forty minutes. Cover art that's legible as a small square on a phone. Links, credits, a page on your site where the episode lives and can be found in search.

None of this is expensive in isolation. All of it is half an hour to an hour per episode, for somebody who writes well. Multiplied by forty episodes, it's a real line in the budget.

## The cost almost nobody budgets for

Now we get to the part that kills more shows than any technical failure: keeping the rhythm after the first month.

In the first few episodes everything is in your favour. The idea is new, internal enthusiasm is high, the first guests are people you already know who say yes immediately, and the material was recorded on a Friday morning when everyone thought it was good fun. By the fourth episode, the enthusiasm has normalised. By the seventh, the list of easy guests has run out. By the tenth, there's a heavy month of client work colliding with the recording day, and the quiet decision to «skip this month» gets taken without anyone announcing it.

The work that sustains a show from month two onwards is almost entirely invisible and almost never appears in the budget:

- **Finding and persuading guests.** Identifying who has something to say, writing the invitation, chasing when they don't reply, rescheduling when they cancel. It's the item that eats the most hours and looks the least like production.
- **Preparing.** Reading or listening to what the guest has already said elsewhere, so you don't ask them the same three questions. Serious preparation takes longer than the conversation itself.
- **Scheduling.** Aligning two or three diaries, a room and an editor. It sounds trivial and isn't.
- **Deciding what comes next.** Somebody has to maintain a list of themes, know where the season stands and notice when the show is repeating itself.
- **Following up afterwards.** Sending the episode to the guest, publishing, replying to the people who comment.

This is a role, not a task. It can be an internal person with protected hours in their diary, or it can be contracted out to somebody who does content production — but it has to have an owner and it has to have time allocated. Shows without a named producer are shows that depend on the goodwill of whoever is least busy that week, and sooner or later that person gets busy.

### Two decisions that reduce the cost of continuing

**Batch recording.** Booking four conversations in a single day divides the fixed cost of the studio, the equipment setup and the mental run-up by four. More importantly, it creates reserve: a show that launches with three edited episodes in the drawer survives an illness, a holiday and one bad month. A show that records on Monday what it publishes on Wednesday lives permanently on the edge of failure.

**Thinking in seasons.** Ten episodes, an announced break, a new season. Budgeting by season is more realistic than budgeting by year, it gives you a natural moment to assess, and it lets you stop without that being a public failure.

## How to build the budget

Instead of asking the price of an episode, do the sum backwards. Define the season: how many episodes, at what length, in what format. Then add up four blocks.

The **setup**, which happens once: sonic identity, theme music, cover art, feed configuration, a page on the site, a first test session. It's a significant figure that gets diluted across every episode that follows — and that is wasted in full if the show stops at the fifth.

The **per-episode production**: studio or equipment amortisation, editing, transcription, clips, publishing.

The **invisible production hours**: guests, preparation, scheduling, follow-up. Estimate these per week, not per episode, because that's how the work actually happens.

The **margin for failure**: the recording that goes wrong and has to be redone, the guest who cancels at the last minute, the track that got lost. It happens at least once a season, every season.

If the total is frightening, the right answer is rarely to cut sound quality or editing. It's to reduce the cadence or shorten the season. Eight well-made fortnightly episodes are worth more than twenty weekly ones that start sliding in quality from the sixth — and they cost less to sustain.

## What you're actually buying

A podcast isn't a set of audio files. It's a commitment to show up regularly for long enough that somebody builds the habit of listening to you. The budget should reflect that: less money on what happens once, more money on what has to happen forty times in a row.

Before you approve the first episode, look at the hardest line in the budget — the hours belonging to whoever will keep this alive in November — and ask whether it's there at all. If it isn't, the show already has an end date; it just doesn't know what it is yet.
