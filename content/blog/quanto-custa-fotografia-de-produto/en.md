When you ask for a product photography quote, you'll almost always get back one of two structures. Either a price per product — so much per item, with a set number of images included — or a price per studio day, with the photographer, the space and the equipment booked for a full working day, and you filling that day as best you can.

At first glance they look like two ways of saying the same thing. They aren't. They're two different distributions of risk. Under per-product pricing, the photographer carries the risk of a slow setup: if that glass bottle fights back for an hour and a half, that's their problem. Under day rate pricing, the risk is yours: if the day yields half of what you expected, you pay the same and go home with half the images.

Understanding this is what lets you compare proposals that, on paper, aren't comparable.

## Pricing per product

In this model, the quote is built from the product list. There's usually a defined package: so many images per item, with specified angles, an agreed background and post-production included up to a certain level. You pay for what you receive, and what you receive is countable.

The advantages are obvious for anyone who has to defend a budget internally. The figure is predictable, it fits neatly into per-product accounting and it scales linearly: add five items and you know exactly what it will cost. It's also the model that works best when you need to top up the catalogue in several batches across the year, because the unit price tends to hold.

The trade-off is rigidity. A per-product price can only exist because someone estimated an average time per item — and that average was calculated assuming products that behave. The moment you step outside what was predicted, the package stops serving you. An extra image, an angle that wasn't on the list, the product open and closed, the colour variant, the packaging alongside it: every addition is a new line, and every new line has a price.

It's also the model that most often produces apparently cheap quotes that grow at the end. It's always worth asking, before you sign, what happens when an item has to be reshot, how many images are genuinely included, and whether post-production covers only cut-out or also cleaning, compositing and colour correction.

## Pricing per studio day

Here you're buying time. A photographer's day, with or without an assistant, with the studio and the equipment set up, and a number of products that depends entirely on how fast things move.

The advantage is flexibility. Within the day, you can decide to shoot more angles of a product that matters more, try two lighting approaches, add a detail view that wasn't planned. There's no conversation about extras, because the unit you're buying is time, and time stretches over whatever you put in front of it.

The disadvantage is that the day's output depends almost entirely on you. A well-prepared day — products cleaned, labelled, in order, with the shot list decided and someone on site to approve — produces a volume of work that surprises people. A badly prepared day burns the morning unwrapping boxes and settling questions that should have been settled weeks earlier, and the client goes home thinking the photographer is slow.

This is the model where the real cost per image can be the lowest on the market or the highest, depending on how organised the person paying is. There's no middle ground.

## Where the break-even point sits

The calculation that matters is simple, and you can do it on your own before you speak to anyone.

Ask the same person for both figures: what the per-product price is and what the day rate is. Then ask how many items, of a type similar to yours, it's reasonable to expect in a day. Divide the day rate by that number and you have the cost per item under the time-based model. Compare it with the per-product price.

The result tends to follow a recognisable pattern:

- **Small catalogues** — a few dozen items or fewer — almost always come out better on per-product pricing. You don't have the volume to fill a full day efficiently, and paying for a whole day to shoot twelve products means paying for time you didn't use.
- **Large, uniform catalogues** — many items of the same type, same size, same lighting setup — almost always come out better on a day rate. This is where repetition works in your favour: the light is built once and the products pass in front of it in sequence. The cost per image drops sharply as the day goes on.
- **Large but heterogeneous catalogues** — a lamp, a garment, a face cream and a chair — don't benefit from a day rate as much as they appear to, because every change of product type means rebuilding the lighting. In these cases, grouping products into families and shooting each family in a block is what recovers the advantage.

There's one variable that throws all of this off balance: uniformity. Twenty identical products are cheaper to photograph than five different ones, regardless of the pricing model. If you can organise your catalogue by similarity of setup, you save money under either scenario.

## What pushes the price up, under either model

Neither model is expensive or cheap in itself. What determines the final figure is the work each image demands, and that work concentrates in a handful of places.

**Difficult products.** Glass, polished metal, mirrored surfaces and cellophane-wrapped packaging are the classic cases. They reflect everything around them, photographer included, and force the lighting to be built with panels and flags rather than simply pointing a light at the subject. A transparent bottle can take longer than ten ceramic pieces.

**Styling.** A product set down on a surface is one thing. A product with props, a chosen surface, a composed arrangement and several attempts until it looks unforced is another. Styling is frequently the line that grows most between the estimate and the final invoice, because it's the one that's hardest to predict in advance.

**People.** The moment a hand, a model, clothing or make-up enters the frame, you're no longer quoting product photography. In come schedules, fees, image rights and a completely different working rhythm.

**Post-production.** This is where most budgets slip. Cutting out against white is fast. Removing dust, specks and scratches, correcting deformed packaging, compositing several exposures to resolve reflections, harmonising colour across forty items so the shop grid doesn't look like a patchwork — that's hours of work, and it has to be written down.

**Movement.** If you want short video or animation from the same setup, say so beforehand. Using lighting that's already built is far cheaper than going back to the studio. Asking on the day, halfway through, tends to be the worst of both worlds.

## The lines that rarely appear in the quote

There are costs that don't belong to the photographer and that are nonetheless part of what the shoot will cost you.

First, the samples. Someone has to gather the products, check they're flawless, clean anything marked and get them to the studio. A scratched, dented product, or one with a crooked label, turns into hours of retouching — or into an image you can't use. It's cheaper to have a fresh unit sent over than to pay for repairs in post-production.

Second, the time of whoever approves. Someone on your side has to be available during the shoot, even if only by message, to decide angles and sign off on framing. Deferred decisions turn into reshoots.

Third, usage rights. For product photography, the norm is a broad licence with no time limit, but it needs to be in writing, particularly if the images are going to resellers, marketplaces or paid advertising. What costs you is never the right itself: it's discovering two years later that you don't have it.

Fourth, the top-ups. New products, updated versions, redesigned packaging. A living catalogue needs replenishing, and it's better to budget a day every six months than to improvise with a phone every time a new item arrives.

## How to ask for a quote you can actually compare

The difference between proposals that seem incomparable is almost always missing information in the question. If you send the same brief to three people with these elements in it, you get three answers you can lay side by side:

- Number of items and, if they're of different types, how many of each type.
- Dimensions and materials, with explicit mention of glass, metal or glossy surfaces.
- Number of images per item and which angles.
- Whether there are context or scale images, and whether they involve people.
- Intended background and level of post-production.
- Final formats and aspect ratios, and where they'll be used.
- Delivery deadline.

With this, any serious quote can tell you what the job would cost per item and what it would cost per day. And once you have both numbers for the same job, the choice stops being a question of trust and becomes arithmetic.

## The number that actually matters

The cost of the shoot is easy to compare and is the least important part. The images will serve the shop, the ads, social media, reseller listings and marketplaces, probably for years. Divide the figure by the time the images will be in use and by all the places they'll appear, and what looked like a one-week expense becomes a cost spread across far more than a photography session.

The expensive mistake isn't choosing the wrong pricing model. It's choosing a quote that doesn't cover post-production, discovering halfway through that angles are missing, and ending up with a catalogue that will have to be redone a year from now.
