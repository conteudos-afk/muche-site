A good brand video doesn't look like an advert. It looks like a window — into a world, a set of values, a way of seeing. The brands that manage this understand something fundamental: the point is not to show what you do. The point is to make the viewer feel what it means to be part of what you do.

The word «cinematic» is usually misused in this conversation. Most people hear shallow depth of field, slow motion, a colour grade with cool shadows. That's a look, and a look can be bought with equipment and hours in post. Cinematic, in the sense that matters, means something else: it means there's an intention behind every shot, that the order in which things appear was decided, that the silence is there for a reason. That's why videos shot with modest means stay with us, and expensive productions evaporate the second they end.

## Story before production value

The most common mistake in brand video is to invest heavily in production value — beautiful photography, expensive locations, immaculate post — and neglect the story. A technically stunning video with nothing to say is forgotten the moment it finishes. A video with a clear, specific, emotionally resonant idea is remembered and shared, even if the budget was small.

There's a simple test we run before moving to any treatment: can you tell someone the video over the phone, without describing a single image? If the answer is «it opens with a drone over the factory at dawn and then the music comes in», there's no story. There's a shot list. If the answer is «it's about a man who learned the trade from his father and is now teaching his daughter, and nobody has ever asked them why», there's a story — and from there you know which shots you need.

Specificity is the engine of all this. Brands tend to climb up to the level of abstraction because everything feels safer up there: innovation, passion, commitment, people at the centre. Those are words that belong to nobody. What belongs to the brand is the strange, concrete detail — the 1974 machine nobody wants to replace because no new one makes that particular cut, the five-in-the-morning shift, the customer who has phoned every week for twelve years. It's always in the detail that a brand becomes recognisable. Abstraction is interchangeable; detail is not.

This also changes how you spread the budget. Two days of filming in a real location, with enough time to build trust with the people in it, will almost always produce more usable material than one day with a crane, big lights and a tight shooting schedule. It's worth asking, line by line, whether each cost serves the story or serves the look.

## The video is made in the brief

An imprecise brief produces an imprecise video. Before any camera starts rolling, you need to be able to articulate four things.

**What is the one thing this video has to communicate?** One. Not three, not «our five business areas». If the video has to explain the entire company, it will communicate nothing. When a client insists on five messages, the agency's job is to help them see that they need five pieces, or a decision.

**Who exactly is it for?** «The general public» is not an audience. A video aimed at candidates for an engineering role is a different video from one aimed at retail buying directors, even if the brand is the same and the underlying story is too. The vocabulary changes, what you show changes, the pace changes.

**What do we want the viewer to feel and then to do?** Both halves of that question matter. The feeling drives the creative decisions — pride, curiosity, relief, a desire to belong. The doing drives the end of the video and the place it will live. A video that ends on an open emotion works at the top of a website; a video that has to generate applications needs to close somewhere else entirely.

**What can we never show or say?** This is the question clients find hardest and the one that saves everyone the most time. Confidential processes, parts of the factory currently under refurbishment, a partner who left, a product line being discontinued in October, a competitor who can't appear in the background. You find this out in the brief, or you find it out at review, with the edit already cut.

The answers to these questions are the architecture of the video. Everything else — locations, casting, music, pace — serves them. And there's a fifth question worth asking, a more uncomfortable one: who signs off? A well-built brief that ends up in front of six people who were never in the conversation always comes back diluted. Better to know who has the final word before you write the first line of the treatment.

## Casting is storytelling

The people who appear in a brand video are its most powerful communicators. They either hold the authenticity up or they destroy it. Three seconds of someone smiling slightly too hard is enough for a viewer to register that this is staged — and from that point on, they distrust everything else.

Real customers and real employees almost always beat actors on brand credibility. They have the right hands, the right posture, the right way of picking up a tool. But they need direction, patience and a controlled environment. In practice, that means three things.

- **Time before the camera.** Half an hour of conversation with nothing recording, about how the person got here and what annoys them about the work, is worth more than any written guidance. By the time the camera is on, you're not meeting a stranger.
- **No memorised lines.** The moment you ask a technician to say «at our company, quality comes first», you've lost them. Ask instead what they can see in a badly made product that nobody else can see. The sentence you want comes right after that.
- **Small audience.** Someone being interviewed with the managing director leaning against the wall will say nothing interesting. Clear the room.

There are cases where actors are the right call: when you need to repeat a scene with precision, when the narrative is openly fictional, when what's required is a physical performance nobody on the payroll can deliver. The mistake is using actors to impersonate real people. Viewers almost always sense it, and the cost of that perception is greater than the gain.

The choice between the authentic and the aspirational — real people versus a constructed ideal — is one of the most important creative decisions you'll make. And it's a brand decision, not a production one: a brand that sells handmade rigour and then shows immaculate models in a suspiciously tidy warehouse is contradicting itself in pictures.

## Music is not a last-minute detail

Music accounts for a disproportionate share of the emotional impact of any video. It shapes how the images are interpreted, sets the pace and triggers memory. The same shot of an empty workshop can be melancholy, tense or proud depending on what's underneath it. The image doesn't change; the meaning changes completely.

Which is why music should be chosen as part of the creative process, not licensed from a library at the end of the edit. There are two practical consequences of leaving it until last.

The first is the temp track problem. You cut to a placeholder, everyone gets used to it, and then the licensed version — or the original composition — always sounds worse by comparison, even when it's objectively better. If you're going to use a temp track, choose one as close as possible to what you'll actually be able to use, or accept from the start that music arrives last and cut to the rhythm of the voice instead.

The second is that the edit and the music build each other. A cut that lands on the beat reads as intention; a cut half a second off reads as a mistake. When you know the music before you cut, you can design the sequence so the instruments come in exactly at the reveal, so the silence falls on the one sentence that genuinely matters. You can't fix that afterwards.

It's also worth deciding early between library and original composition. Libraries work well for contained budgets and short deadlines, as long as you're comfortable with the possibility that another brand is using the same track. Original composition costs more and takes longer, but it gives you a theme you can reuse across everything — the short cuts, the podcast, the transition slide in a presentation. At that point it stops being music and becomes a sonic identity.

## Discipline, not decoration

A cinematic brand video, done properly, is one of the highest-returning investments a brand can make. It works across every digital platform, anchors campaign thinking, and keeps generating value long after the initial media spend. Well planned, it gives you shorter cuts, portraits, pull quotes, recruitment material — provided all of that was thought through before the shoot rather than improvised afterwards.

But it demands the same discipline and the same strategic clarity as any other brand investment. Treat it as production first and strategy second, and you'll end up with a beautiful video that moves nobody.
